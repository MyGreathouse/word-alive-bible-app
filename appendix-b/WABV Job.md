# WABV: Job

*Word Alive Bible Version, draft 1. Words in square brackets, such as \[H-QADOSH\], point to entries in the Biblical Concepts and Language System. Footnotes and centre references are in the appendices at the end. The prologue (1:1 to 2:13) and the epilogue (42:7-17) are prose, and the speeches (3:1 to 42:6) are poetry, set in verse lines. The Hebrew of Job is the hardest in the Old Testament, and many lines are uncertain.*

## Job 1-3: The Prologue and Job's Lament

### Chapter 1

**1** There was a man in the land of Uz whose name was Job. That man was blameless and upright, one who feared God and turned away from evil.

**2** Seven sons and three daughters were born to him.

**3** He owned seven thousand sheep, three thousand camels, five hundred pairs of oxen and five hundred donkeys, and a very large household, so that this man was the greatest of all the people of the east.

**4** His sons used to hold a feast in the house of each one in turn, on his day, and they would send and invite their three sisters to eat and drink with them.

**5** When the days of the feast had gone round, Job would send for them and make them holy \[H-QADOSH\]. He would get up early in the morning and offer a burnt offering for each of them, for Job said, "Perhaps my children have sinned and cursed God in their hearts." This was Job's regular practice.

**6** One day the sons of God came to stand before the LORD, and the adversary \[H-SATAN\] came among them.

**7** The LORD said to the adversary, "Where have you come from?" The adversary answered the LORD, "From roaming the earth and walking about on it."

**8** The LORD said to the adversary, "Have you considered my servant Job? There is no one like him on the earth, a blameless and upright man, who fears God and turns away from evil."

**9** The adversary answered the LORD, "Does Job fear God for nothing?

**10** Have you not put a fence round him and his house and everything he has? You have blessed the work of his hands, and his livestock has spread over the land.

**11** But reach out your hand and strike everything he has, and he will curse you to your face."

**12** The LORD said to the adversary, "Look, everything he has is in your hands. Only do not lay a hand on him." So the adversary went out from the presence of the LORD.

**13** One day, when his sons and daughters were eating and drinking wine in the house of their eldest brother,

**14** a messenger came to Job and said, "The oxen were ploughing and the donkeys were feeding beside them,

**15** when the Sabeans attacked and took them, and killed the servants with the sword. I alone have escaped to tell you."

**16** While he was still speaking, another came and said, "The fire of God fell from the sky and burned up the sheep and the servants and consumed them. I alone have escaped to tell you."

**17** While he was still speaking, another came and said, "The Chaldeans formed three bands and raided the camels and took them, and killed the servants with the sword. I alone have escaped to tell you."

**18** While he was still speaking, another came and said, "Your sons and daughters were eating and drinking wine in the house of their eldest brother,

**19** when a great wind came from across the wilderness and struck the four corners of the house, and it fell on the young people and they are dead. I alone have escaped to tell you."

**20** Then Job got up and tore his robe and shaved his head, and fell to the ground and worshipped.

**21** He said:

"Naked I came from my mother's womb, and naked I will go back there. The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away. Blessed be the name of the LORD."

**22** In all this Job did not sin or charge God with wrong.

### Chapter 2

**1** Again one day the sons of God came to stand before the LORD, and the adversary came among them to stand before the LORD.

**2** The LORD said to the adversary, "Where have you come from?" The adversary answered the LORD, "From roaming the earth and walking about on it."

**3** The LORD said to the adversary, "Have you considered my servant Job? There is no one like him on the earth, a blameless and upright man, who fears God and turns away from evil. He still holds on to his integrity, though you moved me against him to ruin him for no reason."

**4** The adversary answered the LORD, "Skin for skin. A man will give all he has for his life.

**5** But reach out your hand and strike his bone and his flesh, and he will curse you to your face."

**6** The LORD said to the adversary, "Look, he is in your hands. Only spare his life."

**7** So the adversary went out from the presence of the LORD and struck Job with painful sores from the sole of his foot to the top of his head.

**8** Job took a piece of broken pottery to scrape himself with, and sat among the ashes.

**9** His wife said to him, "Do you still hold on to your integrity? Curse God and die."

**10** But he said to her, "You speak like one of the foolish women. Shall we accept good from God and not accept bad?" In all this Job did not sin with his lips.

**11** When Job's three friends heard of all this trouble that had come on him, each came from his own place: Eliphaz the Temanite, Bildad the Shuhite and Zophar the Naamathite. They agreed to meet together to go and show him sympathy and comfort him.

**12** When they looked up from a distance, they did not recognise him. They raised their voices and wept, and each tore his robe and threw dust into the air over his head.

**13** They sat down with him on the ground for seven days and seven nights, and no one said a word to him, for they saw that his pain was very great.

### Chapter 3

**1** After this Job opened his mouth and cursed the day he was born.

**2** He spoke up and said:

**3** "May the day perish when I was born, and the night that said, 'A boy has been conceived.'

**4** May that day be darkness. May God above not look for it, and no light shine on it.

**5** May gloom and deep darkness claim it. May a cloud settle over it, and the blackness of the day terrify it.

**6** As for that night, may darkness seize it. May it not be counted among the days of the year, nor come into the number of the months.

**7** May that night be barren. May no cry of joy come into it.

**8** May those who curse the day curse it, those who are ready to rouse Leviathan.

**9** May its morning stars be dark. May it wait for light and have none, and never see the eyelids of the dawn,

**10** because it did not shut the doors of the womb that bore me, or hide trouble from my eyes.

**11** Why did I not die at birth, come out of the womb and breathe my last?

**12** Why did knees receive me, and why the breasts, that I should suck?

**13** For then I would be lying down and quiet. I would be asleep, and then I would be at rest,

**14** with kings and counsellors of the earth who built ruins for themselves,

**15** or with princes who had gold, who filled their houses with silver.

**16** Or why was I not hidden away like a stillborn child, like infants who never saw the light?

**17** There the wicked stop their raging, and there the weary are at rest.

**18** There the prisoners are at ease together. They do not hear the voice of the overseer.

**19** Small and great are there, and the slave is free from his master.

**20** Why is light given to one in misery, and life to those bitter in soul,

**21** who long for death and it does not come, who dig for it more than for hidden treasure,

**22** who are glad and full of joy when they find the grave?

**23** Why is light given to a man whose way is hidden, whom God has fenced in?

**24** For my sighing comes in place of my food, and my groans pour out like water.

**25** What I feared has come on me, and what I dreaded has happened to me.

**26** I have no ease, no quiet, no rest, and trouble has come."

*Review note: Job 1 to 3 was drafted in WABV wording. The prologue (1:1 to 2:13) is prose and Job's lament (chapter 3) is poetry, set in verse lines. "The adversary" (Hebrew *ha-satan*, with the article) is tagged to the BCLS entry H-SATAN, which was begun in 1 Chronicles 21 and needs to be confirmed. The calamities (1:13-19), the sores (2:7) and Job's wife's words (2:9) are rendered as written, with no explanation added. The Hebrew uses the word "bless" for "curse" in 1:5, 1:11, 2:5 and 2:9, and the footnotes record the matter. The footnotes record the main differences and the uncertain terms. The section needs a check against the Hebrew by a scholar and an originality check before it is locked.*

## Job 4-7: Eliphaz and Job's Reply

### Chapter 4

**1** Then Eliphaz the Temanite answered:

**2** "If someone ventures a word with you, will you be weary? But who can hold back from speaking?

**3** Look, you have instructed many, and you have strengthened weak hands.

**4** Your words have lifted up the one who stumbled, and you have made firm the knees that gave way.

**5** But now it has come to you, and you are worn out. It touches you, and you are dismayed.

**6** Is your fear of God not your confidence, and the integrity of your ways your hope?

**7** Think now: who that was innocent ever perished? Where were the upright ever wiped out?

**8** As I have seen, those who plough trouble and sow harm reap the same.

**9** By the breath of God they perish, and by the blast of his anger they are finished.

**10** The roar of the lion, the voice of the fierce lion, and the teeth of the young lions are broken.

**11** The lion perishes for lack of prey, and the cubs of the lioness are scattered.

**12** A word was brought to me in secret, and my ear caught a whisper of it.

**13** In the unsettling thoughts of the visions of the night, when deep sleep falls on people,

**14** dread came upon me, and trembling, and made all my bones shake.

**15** A spirit \[H-RUACH\] glided past my face, and the hair of my flesh stood on end.

**16** It stood still, but I could not make out its appearance. A form was before my eyes. There was silence, and then I heard a voice:

**17** 'Can a mortal be more righteous than God? Can a man be purer than his Maker?

**18** If God puts no trust in his servants, and charges his angels with error,

**19** how much more those who live in houses of clay, whose foundation is in the dust, who are crushed like a moth.

**20** Between morning and evening they are broken in pieces. Without anyone noticing, they perish for ever.

**21** Is their tent-cord not pulled up within them? They die, and without wisdom.'

### Chapter 5

**1** "Call out now. Is there anyone who will answer you? To which of the holy ones \[H-QADOSH\] will you turn?

**2** For resentment kills the foolish, and jealousy slays the simple.

**3** I have seen the foolish take root, but suddenly I cursed his home.

**4** His children are far from safety. They are crushed at the gate, and no one rescues them.

**5** The hungry eat his harvest, and take it even from among thorns, and the thirsty pant after his wealth.

**6** For trouble does not come out of the dust, nor does hardship spring from the ground,

**7** but people are born to trouble as sparks fly upward.

**8** As for me, I would seek God, and I would lay my cause before God,

**9** who does great things that cannot be searched out, wonders without number:

**10** who gives rain on the earth and sends water on the fields,

**11** setting the lowly on high, and lifting up the mourners to safety.

**12** He frustrates the plans of the crafty, so that their hands cannot carry out their schemes.

**13** He catches the wise in their own cunning, and the plans of the cunning are swept away.

**14** They meet darkness in the daytime, and grope at noon as in the night.

**15** But he saves the needy from the sword of their mouth, and from the hand of the strong.

**16** So the poor have hope, and injustice shuts its mouth.

**17** Happy is the one whom God corrects. So do not despise the discipline of the Almighty.

**18** For he wounds, but he binds up. He strikes, but his hands heal.

**19** From six troubles he will rescue you, and in seven no harm will touch you.

**20** In famine he will redeem you from death, and in war from the power of the sword.

**21** You will be hidden from the lash of the tongue, and you will not fear destruction when it comes.

**22** At destruction and famine you will laugh, and you will not fear the wild animals of the earth.

**23** For you will be in league with the stones of the field, and the wild animals will be at peace with you.

**24** You will know that your tent is secure, and you will visit your home and miss nothing.

**25** You will know that your offspring will be many, and your descendants like the grass of the earth.

**26** You will come to the grave in full vigour, like a shock of grain gathered in its season.

**27** See, we have searched this out, and it is so. Hear it, and know it for yourself."

### Chapter 6

**1** Then Job answered:

**2** "If only my anguish could be weighed, and all my calamity laid in the scales together.

**3** For then it would be heavier than the sand of the seas. That is why my words have been rash.

**4** For the arrows of the Almighty are in me. My spirit drinks their poison. God's terrors are lined up against me.

**5** Does the wild donkey bray when it has grass, or the ox low over its fodder?

**6** Can tasteless food be eaten without salt, or is there flavour in the white of an egg?

**7** My throat refuses to touch it. Such things are like food that makes me sick.

**8** If only my request would be granted, and God would give me what I hope for:

**9** that God would be willing to crush me, to let loose his hand and cut me off.

**10** Then I would still have comfort, and I would leap for joy in unrelenting pain, for I have not denied the words of the Holy One \[H-QADOSH\].

**11** What is my strength, that I should wait? What is my end, that I should be patient?

**12** Is my strength the strength of stones, or is my flesh bronze?

**13** Is there any help in me, when success is driven from me?

**14** A despairing person should have kindness from his friend, even one who forsakes the fear of the Almighty.

**15** My brothers are treacherous like a wadi, like the channels of wadis that pass away,

**16** dark with ice, and swollen with melting snow.

**17** In the dry season they dwindle. In the heat they vanish from their place.

**18** Caravans turn aside from their course. They go up into the wasteland and perish.

**19** The caravans of Tema looked for water. The travelling companies of Sheba hoped for it.

**20** They were ashamed, for they had trusted. They came there and were disappointed.

**21** So you have now become like that. You see something terrible and are afraid.

**22** Did I say, 'Give me something,' or 'Pay a ransom for me from your wealth,'

**23** or 'Save me from the hand of the enemy,' or 'Redeem me from the hand of the ruthless'?

**24** Teach me, and I will be silent. Make me understand where I have gone wrong.

**25** How painful are honest words. But what does your reproof prove?

**26** Do you think you can reprove words, when the speech of a desperate man is wind?

**27** You would even cast lots over an orphan, and barter over your friend.

**28** But now be good enough to look at me, for I will not lie to your face.

**29** Think again; let there be no injustice. Think again; my case is still just.

**30** Is there injustice on my tongue? Can my palate not discern trouble?"

### Chapter 7

**1** "Is not human life on earth a time of service, and are not his days like the days of a hired hand?

**2** Like a slave who longs for the shade, like a hired worker who waits for his wages,

**3** so I have been given months of emptiness, and nights of misery have been counted out to me.

**4** When I lie down I say, 'When will I get up?' But the night drags on, and I am full of tossing until dawn.

**5** My flesh is clothed with worms and scabs of dust. My skin hardens and then breaks out again.

**6** My days are swifter than a weaver's shuttle, and they come to an end without hope.

**7** Remember that my life is a breath. My eye will never again see good.

**8** The eye that sees me will not see me again. Your eyes will be on me, and I will be gone.

**9** As a cloud fades and vanishes, so the one who goes down to Sheol \[H-SHEOL\] does not come up.

**10** He will not return to his house, and his place will not know him any more.

**11** So I will not restrain my mouth. I will speak in the anguish of my spirit \[H-RUACH\]. I will complain in the bitterness of my soul \[H-NEPHESH\].

**12** Am I the Sea, or the sea monster, that you set a guard over me?

**13** When I say, 'My bed will comfort me, my couch will ease my complaint,'

**14** then you frighten me with dreams and terrify me with visions,

**15** so that I would choose strangling, and death rather than my bones.

**16** I loathe my life. I would not live for ever. Leave me alone, for my days are a breath.

**17** What are human beings, that you make so much of them, that you set your mind on them,

**18** that you inspect them every morning, and test them every moment?

**19** How long before you look away from me, and let me alone until I swallow my spit?

**20** If I have sinned, what have I done to you, you watcher of mankind? Why have you made me your target? Why have I become a burden to you?

**21** Why do you not pardon my transgression and take away my guilt? For now I will lie down in the dust. You will seek me, but I will be gone."

*Review note: Job 4 to 7 was drafted in WABV wording. The chapters are poetry, set in verse lines. They give the first speech of Eliphaz (chapters 4 and 5), with the night vision (4:12-21), and Job's answer (chapters 6 and 7). Job's wish to be crushed (6:9) and his prayer to be left alone (7:16-21) are rendered as written, with no softening. The Hebrew is very hard in many places, including 4:2, 4:10-11, 5:5, 5:23, 6:2-3, 6:6, 6:14, 6:25-27 and 7:12, and the footnotes record the matter. Eliphaz's saying in 5:17-18 and his claim in 4:7-8 (the innocent never perish) are among the arguments that the book later tests. The footnotes record the main differences and the uncertain terms. The section needs a check against the Hebrew by a scholar and an originality check before it is locked.*

## Job 8-11: Bildad, Job and Zophar

### Chapter 8

**1** Then Bildad the Shuhite answered:

**2** "How long will you go on saying such things? Your words are a mighty wind.

**3** Does God twist justice? Does the Almighty twist what is right?

**4** If your children sinned against him, he handed them over to the power of their wrongdoing.

**5** If you look for God and plead with the Almighty for mercy,

**6** if you are pure and upright, then surely he will rouse himself for you and restore your rightful home.

**7** Your beginning will seem small, but your end will grow very great.

**8** Ask the former generation, and take note of what their ancestors learned,

**9** for we were born only yesterday and know nothing, and our days on earth are a shadow.

**10** Will they not teach you and tell you, and bring words out of their understanding?

**11** Can papyrus grow tall where there is no marsh? Can reeds flourish without water?

**12** While still in flower and not cut, they wither before any other plant.

**13** Such is the path of all who forget God, and the hope of the godless perishes.

**14** Their confidence is cut off, and what they trust in is a spider's web.

**15** They lean on their house, but it does not stand. They cling to it, but it does not last.

**16** They are green in the sun, and their shoots spread over the garden.

**17** Their roots twine round a heap of stones. They look for a home among the rocks.

**18** If he swallows them up from their place, it denies them: 'I never saw you.'

**19** See, this is the joy of his way, and out of the dust others will spring up.

**20** See, God does not reject the blameless, nor does he take the hand of evildoers.

**21** He will yet fill your mouth with laughter and your lips with shouts of joy.

**22** Those who hate you will be clothed with shame, and the tent of the wicked will be no more."

### Chapter 9

**1** Then Job answered:

**2** "Truly I know that this is so. But how can a mortal be in the right before God?

**3** If one wished to contend with him, he could not answer him once in a thousand times.

**4** He is wise in heart and mighty in strength. Who has hardened himself against him and come out whole?

**5** He moves mountains, and they do not know it, when he overturns them in his anger.

**6** He shakes the earth from its place, and its pillars tremble.

**7** He commands the sun, and it does not rise. He seals up the stars.

**8** He alone stretched out the heavens and trampled the waves of the Sea.

**9** He made the Bear and Orion, the Pleiades and the chambers of the south.

**10** He does great things that cannot be searched out, wonders without number.

**11** Look, he passes by me, and I do not see him. He moves on, and I do not perceive him.

**12** If he snatches away, who can stop him? Who can say to him, 'What are you doing?'

**13** God does not hold back his anger. Beneath him the helpers of Rahab cower.

**14** How then can I answer him, choosing my words with him?

**15** Though I were in the right, I could not answer him. I could only plead for mercy with my judge.

**16** If I summoned him and he answered me, I do not believe he would listen to my voice.

**17** For he crushes me with a tempest and multiplies my wounds for no reason.

**18** He will not let me get my breath, but fills me with bitterness.

**19** If it is a matter of strength, he is the strong one. If it is a matter of justice, who can summon him?

**20** Though I am in the right, my own mouth would condemn me. Though I am blameless, he would prove me crooked.

**21** I am blameless. I do not care about myself. I despise my life.

**22** It is all one, so I say: he destroys the blameless and the wicked.

**23** When disaster brings sudden death, he mocks at the despair of the innocent.

**24** The earth is given into the hand of the wicked. He covers the faces of its judges. If it is not he, who then is it?

**25** My days are swifter than a runner. They flee away and see no good.

**26** They go by like boats of reed, like an eagle swooping on its prey.

**27** If I say, 'I will forget my complaint, I will put off my sad face and be cheerful,'

**28** I am afraid of all my pains. I know that you will not hold me innocent.

**29** I will be condemned. Why then should I struggle in vain?

**30** If I wash myself with snow and make my hands clean with lye,

**31** you will plunge me into the pit, and my own clothes will abhor me.

**32** For he is not a man as I am, that I might answer him, that we might come to trial together.

**33** There is no arbiter between us, who might lay his hand on us both.

**34** Let him take his rod away from me, and not let dread of him terrify me.

**35** Then I would speak without fear of him, for I am not so in myself."

### Chapter 10

**1** "I loathe my life. I will give free rein to my complaint. I will speak in the bitterness of my soul \[H-NEPHESH\].

**2** I will say to God, 'Do not condemn me. Let me know why you contend with me.

**3** Does it please you to oppress, to despise the work of your hands and to favour the plans of the wicked?

**4** Have you eyes of flesh? Do you see as humans see?

**5** Are your days like the days of a mortal, or your years like human years,

**6** that you seek out my guilt and search for my sin,

**7** although you know that I am not guilty, and there is none who can deliver from your hand?

**8** Your hands shaped me and made me, and now you turn and destroy me.

**9** Remember that you made me like clay. Will you return me to dust?

**10** Did you not pour me out like milk and curdle me like cheese?

**11** You clothed me with skin and flesh, and knit me together with bones and sinews.

**12** You gave me life and kindness, and your care preserved my spirit \[H-RUACH\].

**13** Yet these things you hid in your heart. I know that this was in your mind:

**14** if I sinned, you would watch me and not acquit me of my guilt.

**15** If I am guilty, woe to me. If I am in the right, I cannot lift my head, for I am filled with shame and drunk with my affliction.

**16** And if I lift myself up, you hunt me like a lion and again show your wonders against me.

**17** You renew your witnesses against me and increase your anger towards me. Fresh troops come against me.

**18** Why did you bring me out of the womb? I should have died, and no eye seen me.

**19** I should have been as though I had not been, carried from the womb to the grave.

**20** Are not my days few? Let me alone, that I may find a little comfort

**21** before I go, and I will not return, to the land of darkness and deep shadow,

**22** the land of gloom like thick darkness, of deep shadow without order, where light is like thick darkness.'"

### Chapter 11

**1** Then Zophar the Naamathite answered:

**2** "Should a multitude of words go unanswered, and a talker be pronounced right?

**3** Should your babble silence people, and shall you mock and no one shame you?

**4** For you say, 'My teaching is pure, and I am clean in your eyes.'

**5** But oh, that God would speak, and open his lips against you,

**6** and tell you the secrets of wisdom, for sound wisdom has two sides. Know then that God exacts from you less than your guilt deserves.

**7** Can you find out the deep things of God? Can you find out the limit of the Almighty?

**8** It is higher than heaven. What can you do? Deeper than Sheol \[H-SHEOL\]. What can you know?

**9** Its measure is longer than the earth and broader than the sea.

**10** If he passes through and shuts up, and calls an assembly, who can hinder him?

**11** For he knows worthless men, and when he sees iniquity, will he not consider it?

**12** But a hollow man will get understanding when a wild donkey's colt is born a human.

**13** If you prepare your heart, and stretch out your hands to him,

**14** if iniquity is in your hand, put it far away, and let not injustice dwell in your tents,

**15** then you will lift up your face without blemish. You will be secure and will not fear.

**16** You will forget your misery. You will remember it as waters that have passed away.

**17** And your life will be brighter than noonday. Its darkness will be like the morning.

**18** And you will feel secure, because there is hope. You will look around and take your rest in safety.

**19** You will lie down, and none will make you afraid. Many will court your favour.

**20** But the eyes of the wicked will fail. Escape will be lost to them. Their hope is to breathe their last."

*Review note: Job 8 to 11 was drafted in WABV wording. The chapters are poetry, set in verse lines. They give the first speech of Bildad (chapter 8), Job's answer (chapters 9 and 10), and the first speech of Zophar (chapter 11). Bildad's words about Job's children (8:4) and Job's charge that God "destroys the blameless and the wicked" and mocks the despair of the innocent (9:22-24) are rendered as written, with no softening. Job's longing for an arbiter (9:33) and his appeal to God as his maker (10:8-12) are important for the rest of the book. The Hebrew is very hard in many places, including 8:17-18, 9:5-9, 9:13, 9:21, 9:27-31, 10:10-17, 11:3, 11:6 and 11:11-12, and the footnotes record the matter. The footnotes record the main differences and the uncertain terms. The section needs a check against the Hebrew by a scholar and an originality check before it is locked.*

## Job 12-14: Job Answers His Friends and Pleads with God

### Chapter 12

**1** Then Job answered:

**2** "No doubt you are the people, and wisdom will die with you.

**3** But I have a mind as well as you. I am not inferior to you. Who does not know such things as these?

**4** I am a laughingstock to my friends, I who called on God and he answered me, a just and blameless man, a laughingstock.

**5** Those at ease have contempt for misfortune, a shove for those whose feet are unsteady.

**6** The tents of robbers are at peace, and those who provoke God are secure, those who carry their god in their hand.

**7** But ask the animals, and they will teach you, the birds of the air, and they will tell you.

**8** Or speak to the earth, and it will teach you, and the fish of the sea will tell you.

**9** Who among all these does not know that the hand of the LORD has done this?

**10** In his hand is the life of every living thing, and the breath of every human being.

**11** Does not the ear test words as the palate tastes food?

**12** Wisdom is with the aged, and understanding comes with length of days.

**13** With God are wisdom and might. He has counsel and understanding.

**14** If he tears down, it cannot be rebuilt. If he shuts a person in, there is no opening.

**15** If he holds back the waters, they dry up. If he sends them out, they overwhelm the land.

**16** With him are strength and sound wisdom. The deceived and the deceiver are his.

**17** He leads counsellors away stripped, and makes judges fools.

**18** He loosens the bonds of kings, and binds a waistcloth on their loins.

**19** He leads priests away stripped, and overthrows the long-established.

*Review note: Job 12 to 14 was drafted in WABV wording. The chapters are poetry, set in verse lines. They are Job's long answer to the three friends. **1** Then Job answered:

**2** "How you have helped the one who has no power! How you have saved the arm that has no strength!

**3** How you have counselled one who has no wisdom, and made known sound knowledge in abundance!

**4** With whose help have you uttered these words, and whose spirit \[H-RUACH\] has come out of you?

**5** The shades below tremble, the waters and all that live in them.

**8** He binds up the waters in his clouds, and the cloud is not torn open under them.

### Chapter 27

**1** And Job again took up his poem and said:

**2** "As God lives, who has taken away my right, and the Almighty, who has made my soul \[H-NEPHESH\] bitter,

**3** as long as my breath is in me, and the spirit \[H-RUACH\] of God is in my nostrils,

**4** my lips will not speak falsehood, and my tongue will not utter deceit.

**5** Far be it from me to say that you are right. Until I die I will not put away my integrity from me.

**7** Let my enemy be as the wicked, and let him who rises up against me be as the unjust.

**8** For what is the hope of the godless when he is cut off, when God takes away his life?

**9** Will God hear his cry when distress comes upon him?

**10** Will he take delight in the Almighty? Will he call upon God at all times?

**11** I will teach you about the hand of God. What is with the Almighty I will not conceal.

**12** See, all of you have seen it yourselves. Why then have you become altogether vain?

**13** This is the portion of a wicked man with God, and the heritage that oppressors receive from the Almighty:

**14** If his children are many, they are for the sword, and his descendants have not enough bread.

**15** Those who survive him the plague buries, and his widows do not weep.

**16** Though he heaps up silver like dust and prepares clothing like clay,

**17** he may prepare it, but the righteous will wear it, and the innocent will divide the silver.

**18** He builds his house like a moth's, like a booth that a watchman makes.

**19** He goes to bed rich, but will do so no more. He opens his eyes, and it is gone.

**20** Terrors overtake him like a flood. A whirlwind carries him off in the night.

**21** The east wind lifts him up and he is gone. It sweeps him out of his place.

**22** It hurls at him without pity. He flees from its power in headlong flight.

**23** It claps its hands at him and hisses him out of his place."

### Chapter 28

**1** "Surely there is a mine for silver, and a place where gold is refined.

**2** Iron is taken out of the earth, and copper is smelted from the ore.

**3** Miners put an end to darkness and search out to the farthest limit the ore in gloom and deep darkness.

**4** They open shafts far from where people live, forgotten by the foot, hanging far away from mortals, they swing.

**5** As for the earth, out of it comes bread, but underneath it is turned up as by fire.

**6** Its stones are the place of sapphires, and it has dust of gold.

**7** That path no bird of prey knows, and the falcon's eye has not seen it.

**8** The proud beasts have not trodden it. The lion has not passed over it.

**9** Miners put their hand to the flint and overturn mountains at the roots.

**10** They cut out channels in the rocks, and their eyes see every precious thing.

**11** They dam up the streams so they do not trickle, and what is hidden they bring out to the light.

**12** But where shall wisdom be found? And where is the place of understanding?

**13** No human knows the way to it, and it is not found in the land of the living.

**14** The deep says, 'It is not in me,' and the sea says, 'It is not with me.'

**15** It cannot be bought for gold, and silver cannot be weighed out as its price.

**16** It cannot be valued in the gold of Ophir, in precious onyx or sapphire.

**17** Gold and glass cannot equal it, nor can it be exchanged for jewels of fine gold.

**18** No mention shall be made of coral or crystal. The price of wisdom is above pearls.

**19** The topaz of Cush cannot equal it, nor can it be valued in pure gold.

**20** From where then does wisdom come? And where is the place of understanding?

**21** It is hidden from the eyes of all living, and concealed from the birds of the air.

**22** Abaddon and Death say, 'We have heard a rumour of it with our ears.'

**23** God understands the way to it, and he knows its place.

**24** For he looks to the ends of the earth and sees everything under the heavens.

**25** When he gave to the wind its weight and apportioned the waters by measure,

**26** when he made a decree for the rain and a way for the thunderbolt,

**27** then he saw it and declared it. He established it, and also searched it out.

**28** And he said to humankind, 'See, the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom, and to turn away from evil is understanding.'"

## Job 29-31: Job's Last Words

## Job 32-37: Elihu

### Chapter 32

**1** So these three men stopped answering Job, because he was righteous in his own eyes.

**2** Then the anger of Elihu son of Barachel the Buzite, of the family of Ram, burned. He was angry with Job because he justified himself rather than God.

**3** He was also angry with Job's three friends because they had found no answer, and yet had condemned Job.

**4** Now Elihu had waited to speak to Job because they were older than he.

**5** And when Elihu saw that there was no answer in the mouth of the three men, his anger burned.

**6** And Elihu son of Barachel the Buzite answered:

"I am young in years, and you are aged. Therefore I was afraid and did not dare to declare my opinion to you.

**7** I said, 'Let days speak, and many years teach wisdom.'

**8** But it is the spirit \[H-RUACH\] in a person, the breath of the Almighty, that gives understanding.

**9** It is not the old who are wise, nor the aged who understand what is right.

**10** Therefore I say, 'Listen to me. Let me also declare my opinion.'

**11** See, I waited for your words, I listened for your reasonings, while you searched out what to say.

**12** I gave you my attention, and see, there was none among you who refuted Job or answered his words.

**13** Do not say, 'We have found wisdom. God may rout him, not a man.'

**14** He has not directed his words against me, and I will not answer him with your speeches.

**15** They are dismayed. They answer no more. They have not a word to say.

**16** And shall I wait because they do not speak, because they stand there and answer no more?

**17** I also will answer with my share. I also will declare my opinion.

**18** For I am full of words. The spirit \[H-RUACH\] within me constrains me.

**19** My belly is like wine that has no vent. Like new wineskins it is ready to burst.

**20** I must speak, that I may find relief. I must open my lips and answer.

**21** I will not show partiality to any person or flatter any man.

### Chapter 35

**1** And Elihu answered:

**2** "Do you think this to be just? Do you say, 'It is my right before God,'

## Appendix A: Translation Footnotes

*Footnotes sit here, not in the reading text. They are listed by chapter and verse.*

- **Title** Job is a wisdom book about a righteous man who suffers. It has a prose frame (1:1 to 2:13 and 42:7-17) and a long poetic dialogue between (3:1 to 42:6). The date is uncertain. The Hebrew is the hardest in the Old Testament, with many rare words and many lines whose sense is unclear. Job is not an Israelite, and the setting is like that of the patriarchs: wealth is counted in animals, Job offers his own sacrifices, and there is no priest or law of Moses. The divine name (the LORD) is used in the prose frame and in a few other places (12:9; 38:1; 40:1, 3, 6; 42:1, 7-9, 11). In the poetry the names are *Eloah*, *El* and *Shaddai*. The WABV text uses "God" for *Eloah* and *El* and "the Almighty" for *Shaddai*. The form of these should be decided with the BCLS entries.
- **1:1** "The land of Uz" is a region east or south-east of Israel, linked with Edom (Lamentations 4:21; Genesis 36:28). "Blameless and upright" is Hebrew *tam ve-yashar*. It means whole and straight, not without sin. "One who feared God and turned away from evil" is the description repeated in 1:8 and 2:3.
- **1:3** "The greatest of all the people of the east" translates Hebrew *benei qedem*, the people of the eastern desert. The numbers of animals are large.
- **1:4** "Hold a feast in the house of each one in turn, on his day" says that the sons held feasts in rotation. The sisters were invited.
- **1:5** "Make them holy" is Hebrew *va-yeqaddeshem*. See entry H-QADOSH. Job offers burnt offerings for each of his children, as the head of the family. "Cursed God in their hearts" is literally "blessed God" (*berekhu Elohim*). The Hebrew text uses the word "bless" as a way of avoiding the word "curse" in 1:5, 1:11, 2:5 and 2:9. The WABV text gives the sense, "cursed", and the footnote records the Hebrew.
- **1:6** "The sons of God" is Hebrew *benei ha-Elohim*, the heavenly beings of the council of God (see Psalm 82:1 and 89:6-7). "To stand before the LORD" is the verb for presenting oneself for service. "The adversary" is Hebrew *ha-satan*, with the article, so it is a title or a role, "the accuser" or "the adversary". It is not yet a name. See entry H-SATAN. See also 1 Chronicles 21:1 and Zechariah 3:1-2.
- **1:7** "Roaming the earth and walking about on it" suggests an official who goes about the world to see what is happening.
- **1:8** "My servant Job" is the LORD's own word for him.
- **1:9** "Does Job fear God for nothing?" is the question on which the book turns: whether a person can serve God without being rewarded. The Hebrew word *chinnam* means "for nothing" or "without cause". It is used again in 2:3.
- **1:10** "A fence" is Hebrew *sakhta*, from the verb *sakhakh*, "to hedge in". It is protection here. The same verb is used in 3:23, where Job speaks of being "fenced in" by God.
- **1:11** "He will curse you to your face" is literally "he will bless you to your face" (*im lo al panekha yevarkhekha*). See the note on 1:5.
- **1:12** "Everything he has is in your hands" sets the limit for the first testing. "Do not lay a hand on him" protects Job himself.
- **1:13** The four messengers (verses 13 to 19) bring the news of the four disasters, each beginning with "while he was still speaking" and ending with "I alone have escaped to tell you". They are rendered as written.
- **1:15** "The Sabeans" are from Sheba, in south Arabia. They are raiders.
- **1:16** "The fire of God fell from the sky" may mean lightning or a fire from God. The Hebrew does not say which. It is the only natural cause that is called God's.
- **1:17** "The Chaldeans" are tribes of Babylonia, here raiders. They form three bands.
- **1:19** "A great wind from across the wilderness" kills all ten of Job's children. It is told without further comment.
- **1:20** "Tore his robe and shaved his head" are signs of mourning. "Fell to the ground and worshipped" is Job's act in his grief.
- **1:21** "Naked I will go back there" says "there", which may mean the earth or the womb of the earth (see Psalm 139:15 and Ecclesiastes 5:15). "Blessed be the name of the LORD" uses *barukh* in its ordinary sense, in contrast to the use in 1:5.
- **1:22** "Charge God with wrong" translates Hebrew *tiflah*, a rare word that means "something unseemly" or "folly". The sense is that Job did not blame God.
- **2:3** "Though you moved me against him to ruin him for no reason" is the LORD's own statement. The text says that the LORD was moved by the adversary and that the ruin was "for no reason". It is rendered as written, with no explanation. "Integrity" is Hebrew *tummah*, the quality of being *tam*.
- **2:4** "Skin for skin" is a proverb whose meaning is unclear. It may mean that a person will give the skin of another to save his own.
- **2:5** "Strike his bone and his flesh" extends the testing to Job's body. See the note on 1:5.
- **2:6** "Only spare his life" sets the limit for the second testing.
- **2:7** "Painful sores from the sole of his foot to the top of his head" translates Hebrew *shechin ra*, "evil boils". It is the word for the sores of Egypt and of the curses (Exodus 9:9; Deuteronomy 28:35). The disease is not named.
- **2:8** "A piece of broken pottery" is for scraping the sores. "Among the ashes" is the place of the mourner and the outcast.
- **2:9** "His wife" is not named. "Do you still hold on to your integrity? Curse God and die" is her only speech. The Hebrew has "bless God" (see 1:5). The Greek gives her a much longer speech.
- **2:10** "The foolish women" is Hebrew *ha-nevalot*. "Shall we accept good from God and not accept bad?" is Job's answer. "Did not sin with his lips" leaves open whether he sinned in his heart. A later Jewish teaching (in the Talmud, Bava Batra 16b) argues that he did sin in his heart. The Hebrew text does not say so.
- **2:11** "Eliphaz the Temanite, Bildad the Shuhite and Zophar the Naamathite" are three friends. Teman is in Edom. Shuah and Naamah are not certainly located. "Show him sympathy and comfort him" is their purpose.
- **2:12** "They did not recognise him" says that the disease changed his appearance. "Threw dust into the air over his head" is a sign of mourning.
- **2:13** "Seven days and seven nights" is the usual time of mourning (Genesis 50:10; 1 Samuel 31:13). The friends' silence is the only part of their visit in which they say nothing against him.
- **3:1** "Cursed the day he was born" uses the verb *qalal*, "to curse", and not the euphemism of 1:5. Job curses his day and not God. This is the first line of the poetry.
- **3:3** "A boy has been conceived" is Hebrew *hora gaver*. *Gaver* is a strong male child. The poem reverses the creation: darkness, instead of light.
- **3:4** "May God above not look for it" is literally "not seek it". "No light shine on it" reverses Genesis 1:3.
- **3:5** "Gloom and deep darkness" is Hebrew *tsalmavet*, a word that may also mean "the shadow of death". "Claim it" translates Hebrew *yig'aluhu*, "redeem" or "reclaim", from the verb *ga'al*. "The blackness of the day" translates a hard Hebrew word (*kimrire yom*). It may mean "eclipses" or "things that darken the day".
- **3:8** "Those who curse the day" is the Hebrew reading (*orere yom*). Some scholars read "those who curse the sea" (*yam*), because of the next line. "Leviathan" is the sea monster of the old stories (Psalm 74:14; 104:26; Isaiah 27:1). The line speaks of those who could rouse it.
- **3:9** "The eyelids of the dawn" is a figure for the first light.
- **3:10** "The doors of the womb that bore me" is a figure for birth.
- **3:14** "Who built ruins for themselves" translates Hebrew *ha-bonim charavot lamo*. It may mean that they rebuilt ruins, or built tombs, or built places that later fell. The sense is uncertain.
- **3:16** "A stillborn child" is Hebrew *nefel*, and "infants who never saw the light" completes the image.
- **3:17** "The wicked stop their raging" is the sense of Hebrew *rogez*, "turmoil", which also ends the poem (verse 26). The place described is the grave or Sheol (see H-SHEOL), a place of rest.
- **3:18** "The overseer" is Hebrew *noges*, a taskmaster, the same word as in Exodus 3:7.
- **3:23** "Whom God has fenced in" uses the verb *sakhakh* from 1:10. The protection that the adversary mentioned is now, for Job, a trap.
- **3:25** "What I feared has come on me" says that Job's fears were real. It does not say that his fear caused his suffering.
- **3:26** "Trouble has come" ends the poem with the word *rogez*, "turmoil", which opened the picture of rest in verse 17.
- **4:1** "Eliphaz the Temanite answered" begins the first of three rounds of speeches between Job and his three friends (chapters 4 to 27). Eliphaz speaks first and most gently. His argument is that those who suffer have sinned. Each speech is poetry.
- **4:2** The verse is hard in the Hebrew. It may be read "If someone ventures a word with you, will you be wearied?" or as a statement that Eliphaz cannot keep silent.
- **4:3** "You have instructed many ... strengthened weak hands" says that Job had been a teacher and a comforter.
- **4:6** "Is your fear of God not your confidence?" appeals to Job's own piety. The Hebrew order of the line is unusual.
- **4:7** "Who that was innocent ever perished?" is the argument that the innocent do not suffer. The book later tests this.
- **4:8** "Those who plough trouble and sow harm reap the same" is an agricultural proverb of retribution (see Hosea 10:13 and Proverbs 22:8).
- **4:9** "The breath of God ... the blast of his anger" uses the words *nishmat Eloah* and *ruach appo*.
- **4:10** "The roar of the lion, the voice of the fierce lion" uses four different Hebrew words for lions in verses 10 and 11 (*aryeh*, *shachal*, *kefir*, *layish*) and a fifth (*lavi*). Their exact meanings are uncertain. The lions stand for the wicked.
- **4:12** "A word was brought to me in secret" translates Hebrew *yegunav*, "stolen". "A whisper of it" translates *shemets*, a faint sound.
- **4:13** "Visions of the night" begins the account of the night vision (verses 12 to 21).
- **4:15** "A spirit glided past my face" is Hebrew *ruach*. See entry H-RUACH. It may mean a wind or a spirit. The Hebrew does not say.
- **4:16** "A form was before my eyes" says that Eliphaz could not make out what he saw. "There was silence, and then I heard a voice" translates Hebrew *demamah va-qol eshma*. It can also be read "a hushed voice I heard".
- **4:17** "More righteous than God" is Hebrew *me-Eloah*, which can mean "more righteous than God" or "righteous before God". The second sense is the usual one. The question is whether any person can be pure before the Maker.
- **4:18** "His servants ... his angels" are heavenly beings. "Charges his angels with error" translates a very rare word (*toholah*). The sense is that even the angels are not perfect before God.
- **4:19** "Houses of clay" is a figure for the human body (see Genesis 2:7). "Crushed like a moth" can be read "crushed before a moth" (that is, as easily as a moth is crushed).
- **4:21** "Is their tent-cord not pulled up within them?" is a figure of death as the taking down of a tent. The Hebrew word *yitram* may also mean "their excellence".
- **5:1** "The holy ones" is Hebrew *qedoshim*. See entry H-QADOSH. They are heavenly beings. Eliphaz says that no one in heaven will take up Job's cause.
- **5:2** "Resentment kills the foolish" is Hebrew *ka'as*, "vexation" or "anger".
- **5:3** "I cursed his home" is hard in the Hebrew (*va-eqov navehu*). It may mean "I saw his home cursed" or "I pronounced a curse on it".
- **5:4** "Crushed at the gate" says that the children have no one to defend them in court, at the town gate.
- **5:5** "The thirsty pant after his wealth" follows the Hebrew *tsammim*. Some read *tsammim* as "snares". The whole verse is uncertain.
- **5:7** "As sparks fly upward" translates Hebrew *u-venei reshef yagbihu uf*, "the sons of the flame fly high". It may mean sparks or birds of prey. The sense is uncertain.
- **5:9** "Who does great things that cannot be searched out, wonders without number" begins a hymn on the power of God (verses 9 to 16). Similar hymns are in 9:5-10 and 12:13-25.
- **5:13** "He catches the wise in their own cunning" is quoted in 1 Corinthians 3:19.
- **5:15** "The sword of their mouth" is hard. It may mean the sharp tongue or the sword of the powerful.
- **5:17** "Happy is the one whom God corrects" is a saying found in Proverbs 3:11-12, which is quoted in Hebrews 12:5-6. "The Almighty" is Hebrew *Shaddai*.
- **5:19** "From six troubles he will rescue you, and in seven no harm will touch you" is a numerical saying (see Proverbs 6:16 and Amos 1:3).
- **5:23** "You will be in league with the stones of the field" is literally "a covenant with the stones". It means that the stones will not harm the farmer.
- **5:24** "You will visit your home and miss nothing" translates a verb (*lo techta*) that can mean "not miss" (find nothing lacking) or "not sin".
- **5:26** "In full vigour" translates Hebrew *be-khelach*, a rare word that may mean "in ripe old age".
- **5:27** "We have searched this out, and it is so" is the claim of the three friends together.
- **6:2** "My anguish" is Hebrew *ka'asi*, the same word as in 5:2.
- **6:3** "My words have been rash" translates a rare verb (*lau*) that may mean "stammered" or "been rash".
- **6:4** "The arrows of the Almighty" are a figure of God as an archer (see Psalm 38:2). "My spirit drinks their poison" says that the poison reaches his spirit (*ruach*). "God's terrors are lined up against me" is a military figure.
- **6:6** "The white of an egg" translates a rare phrase (*rir challamut*), which may also mean "the slime of purslane", a plant. The sense is uncertain.
- **6:7** The verse is hard. It says that Job refuses food that is tasteless.
- **6:9** "God would be willing to crush me" is Job's wish for death. It is rendered as written.
- **6:10** "The Holy One" is Hebrew *Qadosh*. See entry H-QADOSH. "I would leap for joy in unrelenting pain" translates a hard phrase (*asaldah be-chilah lo yachmol*). The verb *salad* occurs only here. It may mean "I would exult" or "I would harden myself".
- **6:14** The verse is very difficult. It may mean that a friend owes kindness to someone in despair, even if he has given up the fear of the Almighty. The Greek and Syriac read it differently.
- **6:15** "A wadi" is a seasonal stream bed that is dry in summer. It is the image of the friends' unreliability.
- **6:19** "Tema" and "Sheba" are places in Arabia. The caravans looked for water in the wadis.
- **6:21** "You have now become like that" follows the reading tradition. The written text has a different word. The sense is that the friends are like the dry wadi.
- **6:25** "How painful are honest words" translates a hard verb (*nimrtsu*). It may mean "how forceful" or "how painful".
- **6:27** "You would even cast lots over an orphan" translates a hard verb (*tippilu*, "you would cast" or "you would fall on"). It is a figure of cruelty.
- **6:29** "My case is still just" is Hebrew *tsidqi*. See entry H-TSEDAQAH.
- **7:1** "A time of service" is Hebrew *tsava*. It can mean military service or hard labour.
- **7:3** "Months of emptiness" is Hebrew *yarchei shav*, "months of nothing".
- **7:5** "Scabs of dust" translates Hebrew *gish afar*, "clods of dust". The disease is not named.
- **7:6** "A weaver's shuttle" is a figure of the speed of his days.
- **7:7** "My life is a breath" is Hebrew *ruach*, "wind" or "breath".
- **7:9** "Sheol" is the place of the dead. See entry H-SHEOL. "Does not come up" says that there is no return from it.
- **7:11** "The anguish of my spirit" uses *ruach*. See entry H-RUACH. "The bitterness of my soul" uses *nefesh*. See entry H-NEPHESH.
- **7:12** "Am I the Sea, or the sea monster" refers to Yam and Tannin, the chaos monsters of the old stories (see Psalm 74:13 and Job 38:8-11). Job asks why God watches him as he would a monster.
- **7:15** "I would choose strangling, and death rather than my bones" is hard. "My bones" may mean "my pains".
- **7:16** "My days are a breath" is Hebrew *hevel*, the word of Ecclesiastes.
- **7:17** "What are human beings, that you make so much of them" echoes Psalm 8:4, but turns the question into a complaint. The Psalm praises God's attention. Job accuses it.
- **7:19** "Until I swallow my spit" is a proverb for a short time.
- **7:20** "You watcher of mankind" translates Hebrew *notser ha-adam*, "the guard of people". The title is hostile here. "A burden to you" follows the old Greek and the tradition of the scribes, who record that the original read "to you". The Hebrew text most often read has "a burden to myself". The WABV text departs from that reading and is flagged for the scholar.
- **7:21** "You will seek me, but I will be gone" ends the chapter. The verb *shichar* means to seek with care. It may be a last appeal that God will look for him when it is too late.
- **8:1** "Bildad the Shuhite answered" begins the first speech of the second friend. Bildad speaks more sharply than Eliphaz and appeals to the teaching of the ancestors.
- **8:2** "Your words are a mighty wind" says that Job's speech is noisy and empty.
- **8:3** "Does God twist justice? Does the Almighty twist what is right?" is Bildad's central question. The Hebrew words are *mishpat* (justice) and *tsedeq* (what is right). The answer expected is "No".
- **8:4** "If your children sinned against him, he handed them over to the power of their wrongdoing" is rendered as written. It is a harsh saying about the death of Job's children (1:18-19). The Hebrew is literally "into the hand of their transgression".
- **8:5** "Plead with the Almighty for mercy" is Hebrew *tithchannan el Shaddai*. The conditions in verses 5 to 7 offer restoration if Job is "pure and upright" (*zakh ve-yashar*).
- **8:6** "Your rightful home" is Hebrew *naveh tsidqekha*, "the home of your righteousness".
- **8:9** "We were born only yesterday" is an appeal to the limits of human knowledge. "Our days on earth are a shadow" is repeated in 14:2.
- **8:11** "Papyrus" and "reeds" are marsh plants that cannot live without water. They are a figure for the godless.
- **8:13** "All who forget God" is a description of the godless (see Psalm 9:17 and 50:22).
- **8:14** "What they trust in is a spider's web" translates two hard words (*yaqot* and *bet akkavish*). The first may mean "gossamer" or "thread". The sense is that the trust of the godless is thin.
- **8:16** Verses 16 to 19 are very difficult. They seem to describe a plant that is strong in the sun and then is torn out and forgotten. It may be an image of the godless, or of the way their place is taken by others.
- **8:19** "See, this is the joy of his way" is bitter. It may also be read "this is the way of its joy". "Out of the dust others will spring up" says that others will take their place.
- **8:20** "God does not reject the blameless" uses the word *tam*, the word used of Job in 1:1.
- **9:2** "Be in the right before God" is Hebrew *yitsdaq im El*. The verb is a legal one. It means to be found just in a lawsuit with God. This is the question of the whole book.
- **9:3** "He could not answer him once in a thousand times" may mean that God cannot be answered, or that the person cannot. The Hebrew does not say.
- **9:5** The hymn in verses 5 to 10 describes the power of God in creation. It is similar to 5:9-16 and 12:13-25.
- **9:7** "He commands the sun, and it does not rise" may refer to an eclipse. "He seals up the stars" is a figure for darkness.
- **9:8** "Trampled the waves of the Sea" translates Hebrew *doresh al bamote yam*, "treading on the heights of the sea". The Sea (*Yam*) is the old chaos power (see 7:12).
- **9:9** "The Bear and Orion, the Pleiades and the chambers of the south" translates Hebrew *Ash, Kesil, Kimah* and *chadre teman*. They are constellations, and the identification is traditional and not certain (see Amos 5:8 and Job 38:31-32).
- **9:11** "He passes by me, and I do not see him" echoes Exodus 33:22 and 1 Kings 19:11.
- **9:12** "Who can say to him, 'What are you doing?'" is a saying also found in Isaiah 45:9 and Daniel 4:35.
- **9:13** "The helpers of Rahab" are the allies of the sea monster Rahab (see Job 26:12, Psalm 89:10 and Isaiah 51:9).
- **9:15** "My judge" translates Hebrew *meshofti*, which may mean "my opponent at law". "Plead for mercy" is *etchannan*.
- **9:17** "A tempest" is Hebrew *se'arah*, the word used of the storm in which God speaks in 38:1. "For no reason" is *chinnam*, the same word as in 1:9 and 2:3.
- **9:19** "Who can summon him?" is a legal question. Job cannot call God to court.
- **9:21** "I do not care about myself" translates Hebrew *lo eda nafshi*, literally "I do not know my soul". It may mean that he does not regard himself, or that he is beside himself. "I despise my life" is the same sense as 10:1.
- **9:22** "He destroys the blameless and the wicked" is Job's central claim, that God treats the innocent and the guilty alike. It is rendered as written.
- **9:23** "He mocks at the despair of the innocent" translates a hard word (*massat*, "testing" or "despair"). It may mean that God laughs at the trial of the innocent.
- **9:24** "He covers the faces of its judges" says that God blinds the judges of the earth. "If it is not he, who then is it?" ends the verse as a question that Job does not answer.
- **9:26** "Boats of reed" translates Hebrew *oniyyot ebeh*, light papyrus boats.
- **9:30** "Snow" and "lye" are cleansing agents. The washing is a figure for Job's claim to purity.
- **9:31** "You will plunge me into the pit" means a pit or ditch (*shachat*). "My own clothes will abhor me" is a figure of Job's disgust.
- **9:33** "An arbiter" is Hebrew *mokhiach*, a person who can decide between two sides. Job longs for someone who can "lay his hand on us both" and so restrain God. This is the first sign of the hope for an advocate (16:19-21; 19:25).
- **9:34** "His rod" is a figure for God's punishment.
- **9:35** "For I am not so in myself" is hard. It may mean "for I am not so, in myself" (not guilty), or "for I am not like that" (afraid).
- **10:1** "The bitterness of my soul" uses *nefesh*. See entry H-NEPHESH.
- **10:2** "Do not condemn me" is Hebrew *al tarshi'eni*, "do not declare me guilty". "Contend with me" is *tarivni*, the verb of a lawsuit.
- **10:3** "The work of your hands" is language of creation. Job asks why God would despise what he made.
- **10:4** "Eyes of flesh" says that God does not see as humans see.
- **10:8** "Your hands shaped me and made me" begins the picture of creation in verses 8 to 12. It echoes Genesis 2:7.
- **10:10** "Pour me out like milk and curdle me like cheese" is a figure of the formation of a child in the womb.
- **10:12** "Life and kindness" is Hebrew *chayyim va-chesed*. "Kindness" is *chesed*. See entry H-HESED. "Your care preserved my spirit" uses *ruach*. See entry H-RUACH.
- **10:13** "Yet these things you hid in your heart" says that God's purpose was hidden. Job now thinks that it was to watch for his sin.
- **10:15** "Drunk with my affliction" translates a hard phrase (*re'eh oni*), which may mean "seeing my affliction" or "drunk with".
- **10:16** "You hunt me like a lion" is a figure of God as an enemy.
- **10:17** "Fresh troops" translates the Hebrew *chalifot ve-tsava*, "relays and an army". The picture is of a siege with rotating forces.
- **10:18** "Why did you bring me out of the womb?" repeats the lament of 3:11.
- **10:21** "The land of darkness and deep shadow" is Sheol, the place of the dead. See entry H-SHEOL.
- **10:22** "Of deep shadow without order" translates a hard phrase (*lo sedarim*). It says that Sheol has no order. "Where light is like thick darkness" is very difficult.
- **11:1** "Zophar the Naamathite answered" begins the first speech of the third friend. He is the bluntest of the three.
- **11:3** "Your babble" translates Hebrew *baddim*, which may also mean "lies" or "empty talk".
- **11:4** "My teaching is pure" and "I am clean in your eyes" are not words that Job said exactly. They are Zophar's summary of his claim.
- **11:5** "Oh, that God would speak" is a wish that is answered in 38:1, in a way that Zophar does not expect.
- **11:6** "Sound wisdom has two sides" translates Hebrew *ki khiflayim le-tushiyyah*, "for it is double for wisdom". The sense is that wisdom has more than one side. "God exacts from you less than your guilt deserves" follows the Latin and one reading of the Hebrew. The Hebrew may also mean "God forgets part of your guilt".
- **11:7** "The deep things of God" is Hebrew *cheqer Eloah*. "The limit of the Almighty" is *takhlit Shaddai*.
- **11:8** "Sheol" is the place of the dead. See entry H-SHEOL.
- **11:10** "If he passes through and shuts up, and calls an assembly" is hard. It seems to be a picture of a judge who arrests and tries.
- **11:12** "A hollow man will get understanding when a wild donkey's colt is born a human" is a proverb of something that cannot happen. The Hebrew is difficult, and the verse may be a joke.
- **11:14** "If iniquity is in your hand" is a figure for hidden wrongdoing.
- **11:15** "Without blemish" is Hebrew *mi-mum*.
- **11:19** "Many will court your favour" translates Hebrew *yechallu fanayikh*, "many will soften your face".
- **11:20** "Their hope is to breathe their last" translates *mappach nafesh*, "a breathing out of life". It is a figure of death.
- **12:2** "No doubt you are the people, and wisdom will die with you" is sarcasm. Job answers the friends' claim to speak for the wisdom of the ages.
- **12:3** "I have a mind as well as you" is literally "I have a heart (*lev*) as well as you". "I am not inferior to you" translates a verb (*nofel*) that means "to fall". He repeats the claim in 13:2.
- **12:4** "I am a laughingstock to my friends" is Job's complaint. "I who called on God and he answered me" refers to his past, when he prayed and was heard. "A just and blameless man" uses the word *tam* of 1:1.
- **12:5** The verse is hard in the Hebrew (*lappid buz le-ashtut sha'anan*). "Contempt for misfortune" and "a shove for those whose feet are unsteady" is one reading. The word *lappid* usually means "torch", and the line may speak of a torch that is despised by those at ease.
- **12:6** "Those who carry their god in their hand" is hard (*asher hevi Eloah be-yado*). It may mean those who trust their own strength as their god, or those who bring God in their hand as a possession.
- **12:7** The animals, the birds, the earth and the fish are the teachers in verses 7 to 9. The point is that the facts of nature show the hand of God as plainly as the friends' speeches do.
- **12:9** "The hand of the LORD" uses the divine name *YHWH*. It is the only use of the name in the dialogue (3:1 to 37:24). Some Hebrew manuscripts have *Eloah*. The same phrase is in Isaiah 41:20.
- **12:10** "The breath of every human being" translates *ruach kol-besar-ish*, "the breath of all human flesh".
- **12:11** "Does not the ear test words as the palate tastes food?" is a proverb repeated in 34:3.
- **12:13** The hymn in verses 13 to 25 describes God's power to overturn human order. It is similar to 5:9-16 and 9:5-10 and is close to Psalm 107:33-42. Job uses it to say that God's power is also God's way of destroying.
- **12:16** "Sound wisdom" is Hebrew *tushiyyah*, a rare word (see 11:6). "The deceived and the deceiver are his" says that God rules over both.
- **12:17** "Counsellors" and "judges" are officials of the court. "Stripped" may mean stripped of their robes or stripped of their wealth.
- **12:18** "He loosens the bonds of kings and binds a waistcloth on their loins" is hard (*musar melakhim pittei'ach*). It may mean that God frees kings from authority and ties a loincloth on them as slaves.
- **12:19** "Priests" translates Hebrew *kohanim*. Some read it as "princes" (*sarim*). "The long-established" is literally "the permanent ones".
- **12:22** "He uncovers the deep out of darkness" is a figure of God's knowledge of what is hidden.
- **12:24** "A trackless waste" is Hebrew *tohu*, the word of Genesis 1:2.
- **13:1** "My eye has seen all this" begins Job's address to the friends and to God.
- **13:3** "Argue my case with God" is Hebrew *le-hokhiach el Eloah*, "to argue before God". It is the language of a lawsuit. "The Almighty" is *Shaddai*.
- **13:4** "You whitewash with lies" translates Hebrew *tofelei sheqer*, which may mean "you smear" or "you plaster over" with lies. "Worthless physicians" says that they cannot heal.
- **13:5** "If only you would keep silent, and it would be your wisdom" echoes Proverbs 17:28.
- **13:7** "Will you speak falsely for God?" accuses the friends of defending God with arguments that they know are untrue. This is the point on which the LORD judges the friends in 42:7.
- **13:8** "Show partiality towards him" is Hebrew *tissa'un*, "lift the face". It is the language of a judge who favours one side.
- **13:12** "Proverbs of ashes" and "defences of clay" translates a hard word (*gabbim*, "bosses" of a shield). The sense is that their arguments are worthless.
- **13:14** "Take my flesh in my teeth and put my life in my hand" are idioms of taking a great risk.
- **13:15** "I will hope in him" follows the reading tradition (*lo*, "to him" or "in him"). The written text has *lo*, "not", so that the line reads "I have no hope". The Hebrew *yiktelleni* is "he will kill me". The WABV text follows the reading tradition and the footnote gives the written text. "Argue my ways to his face" is *derakhai el-panav okhiach*, again the language of the court.
- **13:16** "This will be my salvation" translates *yeshu'ah*. Job's confidence is that the godless could not dare to stand before God.
- **13:18** "I shall be in the right" is Hebrew *esdaq*. See entry H-TSEDAQAH.
- **13:19** "Then I would be silent and die" says that if anyone can answer him, he would give up.
- **13:20** "Only grant me two things" are the two conditions of verse 21: that God withdraw his hand and that his dread not terrify Job.
- **13:23** "Make me know my transgression and my sin" is Job's demand to be told the charge.
- **13:24** "Why do you hide your face and count me as your enemy?" repeats the complaint of 7:20 and 10:2.
- **13:26** "You write bitter things against me" says that God draws up an indictment. "The iniquities of my youth" refers to sins of the past.
- **13:27** "The stocks" are a device for holding the feet. "You set a limit for the soles of my feet" translates a hard phrase (*al shorshei raglai tithchaqqeh*), which may mean "you mark the soles of my feet".
- **13:28** The verse begins with "he" (*ve-hu*) and may belong with 14:1-2 as the beginning of the next poem. "Rotten thing" is Hebrew *raqav*.
- **14:1** "A mortal, born of woman, is short of days and full of trouble" is a saying about human frailty. "Trouble" is Hebrew *rogez*, the word of 3:17 and 3:26.
- **14:2** "Like a flower he comes up and withers" and "he flees like a shadow" repeat figures of Psalm 90:5-6, 102:11 and 103:15-16.
- **14:4** "Who can bring a clean thing out of an unclean? No one" is a short line (*lo echad*), which may also be read "not one". It refers to the weakness of human beings. Some Greek manuscripts add a line.
- **14:5** "His days are determined" says that the length of life is fixed by God.
- **14:6** "Look away from him and leave him alone" repeats the plea of 7:16 and 10:20.
- **14:7** "For there is hope for a tree" begins the contrast between a tree, which may sprout again, and a man.
- **14:9** "At the scent of water it will bud" is a figure of the revival of a stump.
- **14:10** "Lies helpless" translates Hebrew *yechalash*, "is weakened" or "lies prostrate". "Breathes his last" translates *yigva*.
- **14:12** "Until the heavens are no more, he will not awake" says that the dead do not wake, which is the view of much of the Old Testament. The verse is not a denial of every hope, since verses 13 to 15 imagine something else.
- **14:13** "Oh, that you would hide me in Sheol" is a wish that Sheol be a place of shelter until God's anger has passed. See entry H-SHEOL. "Set me a time and remember me" is a wish for a limit on God's anger.
- **14:14** "If a man dies, will he live again?" is Hebrew *im yamut gever ha-yichyeh*. It is a question and is not answered here. "The days of my service" translates *tsava*, as in 7:1. "My relief" is Hebrew *chalifah*, which may mean "change" or "release" (as at the changing of a guard).
- **14:15** "You would long for the work of your hands" is a very tender line. The verb *kasaf* means to long for, as for a person.
- **14:17** "Sealed up in a bag" is a figure of the way sins were stored in a bag and sealed.
- **14:19** "So you destroy the hope of a person" applies the picture of erosion in verses 18 and 19 to human hope.
- **14:20** "You change his face" refers to the change of death.
- **14:21** "His sons come to honour, and he does not know it" says that the dead do not know what happens to the living.
- **14:22** "He feels only the pain of his own flesh, and mourns only for himself" translates a hard verse. The word for "himself" is *nafsho* (his soul), and the verse may mean that the dead have only a dim awareness of their own state.
- **15:1** "Eliphaz the Temanite answered" begins the second round of speeches (chapters 15 to 21). Eliphaz is sharper than in the first round, and most of his speech describes the fate of the wicked.
- **15:2** "Windy knowledge" is Hebrew *da'at ruach*. "The east wind" is Hebrew *qadim*, the hot dry wind from the desert.
- **15:4** "You are doing away with the fear of God" translates the verb *tafer*, "to break" or "to undermine". "Hindering devotion" translates *tigra siach*, "you diminish meditation" or "prayer".
- **15:5** "Your iniquity teaches your mouth" says that Job's own guilt shapes what he says.
- **15:6** "Your own mouth condemns you, not I" is Eliphaz's charge. Job had used the same phrase of himself in 9:20.
- **15:7** "Were you the first man to be born? Were you brought forth before the hills?" is sarcasm. The second line recalls the way that wisdom speaks of her birth in Proverbs 8:25.
- **15:8** "The council of God" is Hebrew *sod Eloah*, the heavenly council (see 1:6 and Psalm 89:7). "Do you limit wisdom to yourself?" is the charge of arrogance.
- **15:10** "Older than your father" may refer to Eliphaz himself or to others among the friends.
- **15:11** "The consolations of God" refers to Eliphaz's first speech (chapters 4 and 5), which he thinks was gentle. "The word that deals gently with you" translates the rare word *lat*.
- **15:12** "Why do your eyes flash" translates a very rare verb (*yirzemun*). It may mean "wink" or "flash" or "roll".
- **15:13** "Turn your spirit against God" uses *ruach*. See entry H-RUACH.
- **15:14** "What is a mortal, that he should be pure?" repeats the question of 4:17-19.
- **15:15** "His holy ones" is Hebrew *qedoshav*, the heavenly beings. See entry H-QADOSH. "The heavens are not pure in his eyes" repeats 4:18.
- **15:17** "I will show you, listen to me" begins Eliphaz's teaching about the wicked (verses 17 to 35). He says that he draws it from his own experience and from the wise.
- **15:19** "To whom alone the land was given, and no stranger passed among them" says that the wise ancestors were a pure line, with no foreign influence.
- **15:20** "The wicked writhe in pain all their days" is the main claim of the speech. It is a general rule that Eliphaz applies to Job, and Job later answers it (21:7-16).
- **15:21** "The destroyer comes upon them" in "peacetime" says that the wicked are never safe.
- **15:22** "Marked for the sword" translates a verb (*tsafun*) that may mean "stored up" or "watched for".
- **15:23** "They wander about for bread, saying, 'Where is it?'" is a hard phrase (*nodeda hu la-lechem ayyeh*). The word *ayyeh* may also mean "vulture", so that the line reads "he is destined as food for the vulture". The sense is uncertain.
- **15:26** "A thick-bossed shield" is a figure of defiance against God.
- **15:27** "They covered their faces with fat" is a figure of prosperity and arrogance (see Deuteronomy 32:15 and Psalm 73:7).
- **15:28** "In ruined towns" says that the wicked will end in ruins.
- **15:30** "By the breath of his mouth they will be carried off" echoes 4:9.
- **15:31** "Emptiness" is Hebrew *shav*, "nothing" or "falsehood".
- **15:32** "It will be paid in full before their time" is hard. It may mean that their doom will be completed before their natural end.
- **15:34** "Barren" is Hebrew *galmud*. "The tents of bribery" is a figure of the corrupt.
- **15:35** "They conceive trouble and give birth to evil" is a saying found in Psalm 7:14 and Isaiah 59:4.
- **16:2** "Miserable comforters" translates Hebrew *menachamei amal*, "comforters of trouble".
- **16:3** "Windy words" is the same phrase as Eliphaz used of Job (15:2).
- **16:4** "Shake my head at you" is a gesture of mockery. Job says that he could speak like the friends.
- **16:7** "God has worn me out" turns to God. "You have made desolate all my company" translates a word (*adati*) that may mean "my household" or "my community".
- **16:8** "You have shrivelled me up" translates a rare verb (*qemattani*). "It has become a witness" says that Job's wasting is evidence against him, or that it is a witness to his suffering.
- **16:9** "His anger has torn me" begins the picture of God as an enemy (verses 9 to 14). "My adversary" is Hebrew *tsari*. It is rendered as written.
- **16:10** "They have struck my cheek with scorn" refers to people who mock.
- **16:11** "The ungodly" is Hebrew *avil*, "the unjust".
- **16:12** "He set me up as his target" repeats the figure of 7:20.
- **16:13** "His archers surround me" is a military figure. "He pours out my gall on the ground" is a figure of a fatal wound (see Lamentations 2:11).
- **16:14** "Breach upon breach" translates *perets al penei perets*, a figure of a siege.
- **16:16** "Deep darkness" is Hebrew *tsalmavet* (see 3:5).
- **16:17** "There is no violence in my hands, and my prayer is pure" is Job's claim of innocence.
- **16:18** "O earth, do not cover my blood" appeals to the earth not to hide the blood of an innocent person (see Genesis 4:10 and Ezekiel 24:7-8). It is the cry of one who expects to be killed and wants justice.
- **16:19** "My witness is in heaven, and my advocate is on high" translates two Aramaic-like words (*edi* and *sahadi*) for "witness". The Hebrew does not say who this is. It may be God himself, who as witness would speak for Job against the accusations that God seems to make, or a heavenly being. The verses 19 to 21 are among the key lines of the book.
- **16:20** "My friends scorn me" translates a hard Hebrew phrase (*melitsai re'ai*), which may also mean "my intercessor is my friend" or "my interpreters, my friends". The sense is uncertain.
- **16:21** "That he would argue the case of a man with God, as a person does for a neighbour" repeats the longing for an arbiter (9:33).
- **16:22** "I shall go the way from which I shall not return" refers to death and Sheol.
- **17:1** "My spirit is broken" uses *ruach*. See entry H-RUACH. The verb *chubbalah* may mean "corrupted" or "ruined". "The graveyard is ready for me" is a plain statement that he expects to die.
- **17:2** "Mockers" translates Hebrew *hatulim*, "deceivers" or "mockers". "Their provocation" is *hamerotam*, the word of 17:2 and 16:8.
- **17:3** "Give me a pledge now. Put up security for me with yourself" translates a legal phrase (*sima na arvani immakh*). Job asks God to be his own guarantor. The idiom is to "strike hands" in a pledge.
- **17:4** "You have closed their minds to understanding" says that God had blinded the friends.
- **17:5** "He who denounces his friends for a share of their goods" is hard. It seems to be a proverb about a person who betrays friends for gain, and whose children will suffer.
- **17:6** "One in whose face people spit" is a hard Hebrew phrase (*mofet*, from *tofet*). It is the sense of a person who is spat at, or of a byword.
- **17:8** "The upright are appalled at this" says that decent people are shocked by Job's treatment.
- **17:9** "The righteous holds to his way" is a line of confidence. Job says that the righteous person will go on growing stronger.
- **17:10** "But come back, all of you, and come now" is addressed to the three friends. "I shall not find a wise man among you" is Job's verdict on them.
- **17:12** "They turn night into day" is hard. It may refer to the friends who say that the light is near, while Job sits in darkness.
- **17:13** "Sheol" is the place of the dead. See entry H-SHEOL.
- **17:14** "You are my father" and "my mother" and "my sister" refer to the pit and the worm as Job's family.
- **17:15** "Where then is my hope?" is a question that is left open.
- **17:16** "The bars of Sheol" are the gates of the place of the dead. "Shall we descend together into the dust?" asks whether hope will go down with him.
- **18:1** "Bildad the Shuhite answered" begins the second speech of Bildad. It is a long description of the fate of the wicked.
- **18:2** "How long will you hunt for words?" translates a hard phrase (*qintsei la-millin*). The first word may mean "snares" or "hunting", so that the line may read "How long will you set traps of words?" The plural "you" is addressed to Job.
- **18:3** "Why are we counted as cattle?" says that Job has treated the friends as stupid.
- **18:4** "Shall the earth be abandoned for your sake, or the rock be moved from its place?" asks whether the order of the world must change for Job.
- **18:5** "The light of the wicked is put out" is a figure of the end of a family (see Proverbs 13:9 and 24:20).
- **18:8** The verses 8 to 10 list six words for traps (*resheth*, *sevakhah*, *pach*, *tsammim*, *chevel* and *malkodet*). The exact meanings are uncertain. "A snare" in verse 9 is *tsammim*, which is also in 5:5.
- **18:12** "His strength is hungry" is a figure of weakness. "Calamity is ready at his side" translates a hard word (*tsal'o*).
- **18:13** "It eats away parts of his skin" is a figure of a disease. "The firstborn of Death" translates *bekhor mavet*, a personification of a fatal sickness. Death is a figure like the Canaanite god Mot.
- **18:14** "The king of terrors" is Death as a ruler (*melekh ballahot*).
- **18:15** "Fire lives in his tent that is not his" is hard in the Hebrew (*tishkon be-oholo mibbeli lo*). The words "that is not his" may be "there is none of his". "Sulphur" is a figure of destruction (see Genesis 19:24).
- **18:16** "Below, his roots dry up, and above, his branches wither" repeats the figure of the tree (see Malachi 4:1).
- **18:17** "His memory perishes from the earth" is the fate of one who has no children to carry his name.
- **18:19** "He has no offspring or descendant among his people" is a cruel line for Job, whose children have died. It is rendered as written.
- **18:20** "People of the west ... people of the east" translates *acharonim* and *qadmonim*, which may mean "those who come after" and "those who went before" (later and earlier generations), or the west and the east.
- **18:21** "One who does not know God" ends the speech with a description that Bildad applies to Job.
- **19:2** "How long will you torment me and crush me with words?" repeats 18:2.
- **19:3** "These ten times" is a round number for "many times".
- **19:4** "My error stays with me" says that if he has erred, it is his own matter.
- **19:6** "God has put me in the wrong" translates a verb (*iwwetani*) that may mean "has wronged me" or "has twisted me". "His net" is a figure of a trap.
- **19:7** "Violence!" is Hebrew *chamas*, the cry of one who has been wronged. "There is no justice" is *ein mishpat*.
- **19:8** The verses 8 to 12 describe God as a besieging army: walls, darkness, stripped honour, the crown taken, an uprooted tree, and an enemy camp.
- **19:12** "Siege-ramp" is Hebrew *sollah*, a ramp for attacking a city.
- **19:13** The verses 13 to 19 list the people who have turned away: brothers, acquaintances, relatives, close friends, guests, maids, servants, his wife, his family and children, and his intimate friends. This is the loss of all human connection.
- **19:17** "My breath is strange to my wife" says that Job's breath is repulsive to her. "My own family" is literally "the sons of my belly", which may mean his brothers or his children.
- **19:18** "Young children" translates Hebrew *avilim*, which may mean "boys" or "the wicked".
- **19:20** "I have escaped by the skin of my teeth" is a proverb of uncertain meaning. Job has nothing left but his gums.
- **19:21** "Have pity on me, have pity on me" is the only direct appeal that Job makes to the friends for mercy.
- **19:22** "Why do you pursue me as God does?" says that the friends act like God against Job. "Satisfied with my flesh" is a figure of devouring, as in slander.
- **19:23** "Oh, that my words were written!" begins a wish for a lasting record of Job's claim. It is the beginning of a passage that is among the most discussed in the Old Testament.
- **19:24** "An iron pen and lead" may refer to a way of cutting letters in stone and filling them with lead. The inscription would be a permanent record.
- **19:25** "My redeemer" is Hebrew *go'el*. See entry H-GOEL. The *go'el* is the kinsman who buys back, defends or avenges a relative (Leviticus 25:25; Ruth 2:20; Numbers 35:19). Here it is a person who will stand for Job. It may be God, or a heavenly being, and the Hebrew does not say which. "Lives" says that the redeemer is alive now. "At the last" translates *acharon*, which may mean "last" in time (at the end of Job's life or of history) or "the last one" who has the final word. "He will stand" uses the verb *yaqum*, the legal verb for rising to speak in court. "On the dust" translates *al afar*, which may mean "on the earth", "on the dust" (of the grave) or "over my dust".
- **19:26** "After my skin has been stripped away" translates a very hard phrase (*acharei ori niqqefu zot*). "From my flesh I will see God" translates *u-mi-besari echezeh Eloah*. The word *mi* can mean "from", "out of", "in" or "without", so that the line may mean "in my flesh" or "without my flesh". The verse has been read as hope of vindication in this life, as hope of seeing God after death, and as hope of a bodily resurrection. The WABV text keeps the ambiguity as far as the English allows. The Latin Vulgate has "in my flesh I shall see my God", which has influenced many translations.
- **19:27** "On my side" translates Hebrew *li*, "for myself" or "on my side". "And not a stranger" translates *ve-lo zar*, "and not an enemy" or "not another". "My heart wastes away within me" is literally "my kidneys fail within me" (*kilyotai*). The kidneys are the seat of deep emotions.
- **19:28** "The root of the matter is found in him" follows the Hebrew *bo*, "in him". Many manuscripts have "in me" (*bi*). The line gives the friends' charge that the source of the trouble is in Job.
- **19:29** "Be afraid of the sword" is a warning to the friends that God will judge them. "That you may know there is a judgement" translates *shaddun* in the written text and *shaddayin* in other readings. The word may also mean "that you may know the Almighty". The sense is uncertain.
- **20:1** "Zophar the Naamathite answered" begins the second speech of Zophar. It describes the short triumph of the wicked.
- **20:2** "My thoughts answer me because of my haste within me" is hard. It says that Zophar is moved to speak quickly.
- **20:3** "A spirit answers me" uses *ruach*. See entry H-RUACH. "Out of my understanding" may mean from his own mind.
- **20:4** "Since man was placed on earth" is an appeal to the oldest experience.
- **20:6** "His height mounts up to the heavens" is a figure of pride (see Genesis 11:4).
- **20:7** "Like his own dung" translates Hebrew *ke-gelelo*, a rare word for dung.
- **20:10** "His children will seek favour from the poor" says that his children will become poor and ask the poor for help.
- **20:12** The verses 12 to 16 are an image of food: evil is sweet in the mouth, but becomes poison in the stomach. The wicked swallow riches, but they are vomited up.
- **20:14** "The venom of cobras" translates Hebrew *merorat petanim*. "Cobras" is *petanim* and "viper" (verse 16) is *ef'eh*.
- **20:17** "The streams flowing with honey and curds" is a figure of paradise (see Exodus 3:8 and Deuteronomy 32:13-14).
- **20:19** "He has crushed and abandoned the poor" is the charge of the speech. "A house that he did not build" says that he has taken what is not his.
- **20:20** "Contentment in his belly" translates *shalev*, a word for quiet or peace.
- **20:21** "There was nothing left after he had eaten" translates *ein sarid le-okhlo*, which may mean that he left nothing for anyone else.
- **20:23** "God will send his burning anger into him and rain it down on him as his food" is rendered as written.
- **20:24** "A bronze arrow" translates Hebrew *qeshet nechushah*, "a bronze bow", which is a bow with a bronze arrow.
- **20:25** "His gall" is Hebrew *merorah*.
- **20:26** "A fire not fanned" is a fire that no one lit, which may be God's fire.
- **20:27** "The heavens will reveal his iniquity, and the earth will rise up against him" makes the heavens and the earth witnesses (see Deuteronomy 32:1 and Isaiah 1:2).
- **20:28** "His goods will flow away" translates a verb (*yigleh*, "will be carried off") that may be "flood away".
- **20:29** "This is the wicked man's portion from God" closes the speech with the words of 27:13.
- **21:2** "Listen carefully to my words, and let this be the consolation you give" turns the friends' task of comfort into a request that they listen. "Mock on" (verse 3) says that they may mock when he has finished.
- **21:4** "Is my complaint against a human?" says that his complaint is against God.
- **21:5** "Lay your hand on your mouth" is a gesture of silence (see 29:9 and 40:4).
- **21:7** "Why do the wicked live, grow old, and become mighty in power?" is Job's direct answer to Eliphaz (15:20) and Bildad (18:5-21). The verses 7 to 16 describe the wicked who prosper.
- **21:9** "No rod of God is upon them" says that the wicked are not punished.
- **21:10** "Their bull breeds without fail" refers to the success of their herds.
- **21:13** "Go down to Sheol in peace" is the reading of the old versions. The Hebrew text has *u-vrega Sheol yechatu*, "in a moment they go down to Sheol", which may also mean "they are terrified". The WABV text follows the old versions and is flagged for the scholar. See entry H-SHEOL.
- **21:14** "Leave us alone. We do not desire to know your ways" is what the wicked say to God. Eliphaz repeats the words in 22:17.
- **21:15** "Who is the Almighty, that we should serve him?" is like Pharaoh's words in Exodus 5:2.
- **21:16** "Their prosperity is not in their hand" is hard. It may be Job's concession that it is God who gives it. "The counsel of the wicked is far from me" is repeated by Eliphaz in 22:18.
- **21:17** "How often is the lamp of the wicked put out?" answers Bildad's claim in 18:5-6 by asking how seldom it happens.
- **21:19** "God stores up their iniquity for their children" is the friends' teaching (see Exodus 20:5). Job replies that God should punish the wicked person, not the children (see Ezekiel 18:2-4).
- **21:22** "Will anyone teach God knowledge?" is a rebuke to the friends for presuming to explain God's ways.
- **21:24** "His pails full of milk" translates a rare word (*atinav*), which may mean "pails", "loins" or "sides". The sense is a picture of health.
- **21:25** "Bitterness of soul" uses *nefesh*. See entry H-NEPHESH.
- **21:26** "They lie down alike in the dust" is Job's conclusion that death makes the rich and the poor the same.
- **21:28** "Where is the house of the prince?" is the friends' question, which expects the answer that the wicked are destroyed.
- **21:29** "Those who travel the roads" are people who have seen the world and can testify.
- **21:32** "Watch is kept over his tomb" says that the wicked have an honoured burial.
- **21:33** "The clods of the valley are sweet to him" says that the earth lies lightly on him.
- **21:34** "Empty nothings" translates *hevel*. "Falsehood" translates *ma'al*, a word of treachery.
- **22:1** "Eliphaz the Temanite answered" begins the third round of speeches (chapters 22 to 27). Eliphaz now accuses Job of particular sins.
- **22:2** "Can a man be of use to God?" says that God does not need human goodness.
- **22:4** "Is it for your fear of him that he reproves you?" is sarcasm. It says that God has not reproved Job for his piety.
- **22:5** "Is not your evil great? There is no end to your iniquities" is a charge that Eliphaz does not support with any evidence.
- **22:6** The charges in verses 6 to 9 are that Job took pledges without cause, stripped the poor, gave no water or bread, and sent away widows and orphans. They are the first named wrongs in the dialogue. Job denies them in chapter 31. They are rendered as written.
- **22:8** "The man with power possessed the land" may refer to Job, or to a powerful person that Job favoured. The Hebrew is very short.
- **22:10** "Therefore snares are around you" begins the description of Job's punishment.
- **22:13** "What does God know?" is what Eliphaz says that Job has said. Job did not say this in these words.
- **22:14** "The vault of heaven" is Hebrew *chug shamayim*, the circle of the sky (see Isaiah 40:22).
- **22:15** "The old way that wicked men have trodden" may refer to the generation of the flood (see Genesis 6 to 8). "Their foundation was washed away like a river" fits the flood.
- **22:17** "Leave us alone" repeats 21:14.
- **22:18** "The counsel of the wicked is far from me" quotes Job's words in 21:16, as a retort.
- **22:20** "Our adversaries are cut off" is the speech of the righteous who see the wicked destroyed.
- **22:21** "Agree with God" translates Hebrew *hasken*, "become familiar" or "be of use". The advice of verses 21 to 30 is the offer of restoration.
- **22:23** "If you return to the Almighty" is Hebrew *im tashuv ad Shaddai*. See entry H-SHUV. "You will be built up" is a figure of restoration.
- **22:24** "Lay gold in the dust" is an instruction to give up wealth. "Gold of Ophir" is the best gold.
- **22:25** "The Almighty will be your gold" translates a word (*betser*) that may mean "ore" or "gold". "Precious silver" is a rare phrase (*kesef to'afot*), which may mean "silver in heaps".
- **22:29** "It is pride" translates a hard word (*gevah*). "He saves the humble" is literally "the one with downcast eyes".
- **22:30** "He will deliver even the one who is not innocent" translates Hebrew *i naqi*, which may also be "the island of the innocent". The sense is that the friend's own innocence can save others. In 42:8 Job prays for his friends.
- **23:2** "His hand is heavy" follows the Greek and Syriac. The Hebrew text has "my hand". The WABV text departs from the Hebrew reading and is flagged for the scholar.
- **23:3** "His dwelling" translates Hebrew *tekhunato*, "his seat" or "his place".
- **23:6** "No, he would pay attention to me" is hard in the Hebrew. It says that God would not crush him, but would listen.
- **23:7** "From my judge" is *mi-shofti*. Job thinks that in a fair hearing he would be freed.
- **23:8** The verses 8 and 9 name four directions (forward, backward, left and right), and God is in none of them.
- **23:10** "When he has tested me, I shall come out as gold" is a figure of refining (see Psalm 66:10, Zechariah 13:9 and 1 Peter 1:7).
- **23:12** "More than my daily bread" translates Hebrew *mi-chuqqi*, which may mean "more than my portion" or "in my bosom" (*be-cheqi*). The sense is that Job treasured God's words more than food.
- **23:13** "He is unique" translates Hebrew *be-echad*, "he is in one", which may mean "he is of one mind".
- **23:14** "He will complete what he appoints for me" says that God's plan will be carried out.
- **23:17** "I am not silenced by the darkness" translates a hard verb (*nitsmatti*). It may also be read "I was not cut off before the darkness".
- **24:1** "Why are times not stored up by the Almighty?" is hard in the Hebrew. It asks why God does not set times for judgement, so that those who know him might see his days.
- **24:2** "The wicked move boundary markers" is a crime of the law (Deuteronomy 19:14; 27:17; Proverbs 22:28).
- **24:3** "The widow's ox as a pledge" is against the law (Exodus 22:26; Deuteronomy 24:17).
- **24:5** "Like wild donkeys in the desert" is a figure of the poor who must find food in the wilderness.
- **24:6** "They gather their fodder in the field" is uncertain. The Hebrew may mean that the poor reap another man's field and glean the vineyard.
- **24:9** The verse about the orphan snatched from the breast seems out of place and may belong after verse 3. The WABV text keeps it in order and the footnote records the matter.
- **24:12** "The dying groan" translates Hebrew *metim*, which may be "people" or "the dying". "The soul of the wounded" uses *nefesh*. See entry H-NEPHESH. "God does not charge anyone with wrongdoing" translates a hard phrase (*Eloah lo yasim tiflah*), which may also mean "God pays no attention to the prayer". The word *tiflah* is also in 1:22.
- **24:13** "There are those who rebel against the light" begins a picture of murderers, adulterers and thieves who work in the dark.
- **24:15** "The eye of the adulterer waits for the twilight" is a saying found in Proverbs 7:9.
- **24:16** "They dig through houses" is a picture of burglary (see Exodus 22:2).
- **24:17** "Deep darkness" is *tsalmavet*.
- **24:18** The verses 18 to 24 are very difficult. They state that the wicked are punished, which is the friends' view and not Job's. Many scholars think that these verses are part of a speech of one of the friends, perhaps the missing third speech of Zophar. The same is true of 27:13-23. The WABV text keeps them where they stand.
- **24:19** "Sheol" is the place of the dead. See entry H-SHEOL.
- **24:20** "The worm finds them sweet" is a figure of death.
- **24:22** "God drags away the mighty by his power" may be a statement of God's judgement on the mighty.
- **24:25** "Who will prove me a liar?" ends the chapter with a challenge.
- **25:1** "Bildad the Shuhite answered" begins the third speech of Bildad. It has only six verses, and Zophar does not speak in the third round. Many scholars think that the order of the third round has been disturbed (see the notes on 24:18, 26:5 and 27:13).
- **25:2** "Dominion and dread belong to God" begins a short hymn to God's greatness. "He makes peace in his high places" is a figure of order among the heavenly beings.
- **25:3** "His armies" translates Hebrew *gedudav*, the hosts of heaven. "On whom does his light not rise?" says that God's rule is for all.
- **25:4** "How then can a mortal be righteous before God?" repeats the questions of 4:17 and 15:14.
- **25:5** "Even the moon is not bright" translates a verb (*ya'ahil*) that may also mean "does not shine" or "does not pitch its tent". The moon and stars are not pure before God.
- **25:6** "A maggot ... a worm" are the words *rimmah* and *tole'ah*, which are creatures of the grave.
- **26:2** "How you have helped the one who has no power!" is sarcasm. The "you" is singular and is probably addressed to Bildad.
- **26:4** "Whose spirit has come out of you?" uses *ruach*. See entry H-RUACH. Job asks who inspired Bildad's words.
- **26:5** The verses 5 to 14 are a hymn to God's power over the dead and the sea. In the Hebrew the speaker is Job. Many scholars think that these verses are part of Bildad's third speech (25:1-6), because they match its style, and that Job's reply is in verses 1 to 4 and in chapter 27. The WABV text keeps the Hebrew order. "The shades" are Hebrew *refa'im*, the dead. The next line ("the waters and all that live in them") is hard in the Hebrew.
- **26:6** "Sheol is naked before God" says that the place of the dead is open to him. See entry H-SHEOL. "Abaddon" is a Hebrew word for "destruction", a name for the place of the dead (see Proverbs 15:11 and Psalm 88:11).
- **26:7** "The north" is Hebrew *tsafon*, the mountain of the north in the old stories, or the northern sky. "The void" is *tohu*, the word of Genesis 1:2. "Hangs the earth on nothing" is a remarkable line of poetry.
- **26:9** "The full moon" follows the reading *keseh*. The written text is *kisseh*, "throne", so that the line may read "He covers the face of his throne". The sense is uncertain.
- **26:10** "He has drawn a circle on the face of the waters" refers to the horizon (see Proverbs 8:27). "At the boundary between light and darkness" says where light and darkness meet.
- **26:11** "The pillars of heaven" may be mountains that appear to hold the sky.
- **26:12** "He stilled the Sea" translates a verb (*raga*) that may mean "stilled" or "stirred up". "Rahab" is the chaos monster (see 9:13, Psalm 89:10 and Isaiah 51:9).
- **26:13** "By his breath the heavens were made fair" is hard (*be-ruchō shamayim shifrah*). "The fleeing serpent" is a sea monster (see Isaiah 27:1).
- **26:14** "The outskirts of his ways" means only the edges of what God does. "How small a whisper" translates *shemets davar*, "a whisper of a word". "The thunder of his power" says that the whole is beyond understanding.
- **27:1** "Took up his poem" translates Hebrew *va-yosef Iyyov se'et meshalo*, "Job went on lifting up his poem". The word *mashal* is a formal speech.
- **27:2** "As God lives" is an oath formula (*chai El*). Job swears by the God who has "taken away my right" (*mishpat*). "Made my soul bitter" uses *nefesh*. See entry H-NEPHESH.
- **27:3** "My breath is in me, and the spirit of God is in my nostrils" refers to Genesis 2:7 and to 33:4. "Spirit" is *ruach*. See entry H-RUACH.
- **27:5** "Far be it from me to say that you are right" is addressed to the friends. The verb *tsadaq* means to declare someone in the right. "Integrity" is *tummah*, as in 2:3 and 2:9.
- **27:6** "My heart does not reproach me for any of my days" is Job's claim of a clear conscience.
- **27:7** "Let my enemy be as the wicked" is a curse on those who oppose Job. It may also be read as a statement that those who oppose him are in effect the wicked.
- **27:8** "What is the hope of the godless when he is cut off?" begins a passage (verses 8 to 10) about the end of the godless. It is hard to say whether Job means that the godless have no hope, or whether he is quoting the friends.
- **27:11** "The hand of God" is God's way of acting. "What is with the Almighty" is God's purposes.
- **27:12** "Altogether vain" translates *hevel*, as in 21:34.
- **27:13** The verses 13 to 23 describe the end of the wicked, which is the view of the friends and not of Job. Many scholars think that these verses are a part of a speech of Zophar, who has no third speech (see the note on 24:18). The words "This is the portion of a wicked man with God" are the same as in 20:29. The WABV text keeps the Hebrew order and the Hebrew attribution to Job.
- **27:14** "If his children are many, they are for the sword" is a hard line. It describes the loss of the children of the wicked.
- **27:15** "The plague buries" translates *mavet*, "death". "His widows do not weep" means that there is no mourning.
- **27:16** "Silver like dust and clothing like clay" is a figure of great wealth.
- **27:17** "The righteous will wear it" says that the wealth passes to the innocent.
- **27:18** "He builds his house like a moth's" follows the Hebrew *ash*. The Greek has "a spider's" (see 8:14). "A booth that a watchman makes" is a hut of branches for a short stay.
- **27:19** "He goes to bed rich, but will do so no more" translates a hard phrase (*lo ye'asef*, "is not gathered"). It may also mean that he is not gathered to his family in burial.
- **27:21** "The east wind" is the hot desert wind (see 15:2).
- **27:23** "It claps its hands at him and hisses him out of his place" is a figure of mockery.
- **28:1** The poem in chapter 28 is a hymn on wisdom. It is not in the style of the speeches of the dialogue and may be a separate poem. It has three parts: the miners' search (verses 1 to 11), the fact that wisdom cannot be bought or found (verses 12 to 19), and the fact that only God knows it (verses 20 to 28). The refrain "Where shall wisdom be found?" is in verses 12 and 20. "A mine for silver" translates *moza*, a place of going out.
- **28:2** "Copper" is Hebrew *nechushah*. The metals are named in the order of the ancient mines.
- **28:3** "Miners put an end to darkness" says that miners carry light into the dark.
- **28:4** The verse is very difficult. It describes shafts cut far from where people live, where the miners hang on ropes. The Hebrew words *nachal* ("shaft" or "valley") and *me-im ger* ("far from where people live") are uncertain.
- **28:5** "Out of it comes bread, but underneath it is turned up as by fire" says that the same earth has fields above and fire (or mining) below.
- **28:7** "Bird of prey" is Hebrew *ayit* and "falcon" is *ayyah*. They are birds with sharp sight. The path of the miners is hidden even from them.
- **28:8** "The proud beasts" is literally "the sons of pride" (*benei shachats*).
- **28:9** "Flint" is Hebrew *challamish*, hard rock.
- **28:12** "But where shall wisdom be found?" is the question of the poem. The Hebrew word for wisdom is *chokhmah*.
- **28:13** "The way to it" follows the Greek. The Hebrew text has *erkah*, "its price" or "its worth". The WABV text departs from the Hebrew reading and is flagged for the scholar.
- **28:14** "The deep" is *tehom*, the ocean below the earth. Neither the deep nor the sea has wisdom.
- **28:15** "It cannot be bought for gold" begins a list of precious things (verses 15 to 19) that cannot buy wisdom.
- **28:16** "Onyx" is Hebrew *shoham*. The identification of this stone and of the others in verses 16 to 19 is traditional and is uncertain.
- **28:17** "Glass" is Hebrew *zekhukhit*. In the ancient world glass was as costly as gold.
- **28:18** "Coral" (*ramot*), "crystal" (*gavish*) and "pearls" (*peninim*) are traditional renderings. The words may refer to other stones.
- **28:19** "The topaz of Cush" is Hebrew *pitdat Kush*, a stone from Ethiopia.
- **28:22** "Abaddon and Death say, 'We have heard a rumour of it with our ears'" says that even the realm of the dead has only heard about wisdom.
- **28:23** "God understands the way to it" is the answer to the question. "He knows its place" says that wisdom is God's alone.
- **28:25** "When he gave to the wind its weight" refers to the work of creation, as in Proverbs 8:22-31.
- **28:27** "Then he saw it and declared it. He established it, and also searched it out" says that God saw wisdom at the creation and set it in the world. The four verbs (*ra'ah*, *sipperah*, *hekhinah* and *chaqarah*) describe his acts.
- **28:28** "The fear of the Lord, that is wisdom" uses *Adonai* ("Lord") and not the divine name. Many Hebrew manuscripts read *YHWH* or *Eloah*. The line is the same as in Proverbs 1:7 and 9:10, with a different word for "Lord". "To turn away from evil is understanding" repeats the description of Job in 1:1, 1:8 and 2:3. The verse is the answer to the question of verses 12 and 20.
- **29:1** "Job again took up his poem" begins Job's last speech (chapters 29 to 31), which is a monologue and ends with "The words of Job are ended" (31:40). It has three parts: his old life (29), his present shame (30), and his oath (31).
- **29:2** "The months of old" are the time before the disaster. "When God watched over me" translates the verb *shamar*, "to guard".
- **29:4** "The days of my prime" translates a rare word (*charpi*, "my autumn"), which may mean the time of harvest, the best of life. "The friendship of God" is Hebrew *sod Eloah*. The word *sod* means a close circle or intimacy (see Psalm 25:14).
- **29:5** "My children were around me" says that his family was still alive. The Hebrew word (*ne'arai*) may mean "my children" or "my young servants".
- **29:6** "Cream" is Hebrew *chem'ah*. "The rock poured out streams of oil" is a figure of the olive press and of great wealth.
- **29:7** "The gate of the city" and "the square" were the place of the court and of public life.
- **29:9** "Laid their hand on their mouth" is a sign of respect and silence (see 21:5 and 40:4).
- **29:11** "It blessed me" and "approved me" are the response of those who heard of Job's deeds.
- **29:14** "I put on righteousness, and it clothed me" is Hebrew *tsedeq* (see entry H-TSEDAQAH). "My justice" is *mishpat*. "A turban" translates *tsanif*, a headdress of a person of rank.
- **29:16** "I was a father to the needy" is a claim that he protected the poor. "I searched out the cause of the stranger" says that he took up the case of those whom he did not know.
- **29:17** "I broke the fangs of the unjust" is a figure of the lion's teeth (see 4:10).
- **29:18** "I shall die in my nest" is a figure of a secure end. "Like the sand" translates Hebrew *chol*, which may also mean "the phoenix", a bird that was thought to live very long. The Greek has "palm tree". The sense is a long life.
- **29:21** "People listened to me and waited" begins the memory of Job's counsel. His word "dropped on them like dew" (verse 22).
- **29:24** "I smiled on them when they had no confidence" says that Job gave heart to those who had lost it. The second line ("the light of my face they did not cast down") is hard.
- **29:25** "Like a king among his troops, like one who comforts mourners" is a figure of a leader who is also gentle.
- **30:1** "But now they laugh at me" turns from the old days to the present. "The dogs of my flock" are the sheepdogs, and the figure is of men too low to be set with them.
- **30:2** "Their vigour" is Hebrew *kelach*, a rare word (see 5:26).
- **30:3** "They gnaw the dry ground by night" translates a hard phrase (*ha-orqim tsiyyah*). The figure is of the poor who search the desert for food.
- **30:4** "Mallow" is Hebrew *malluach*, a salty plant. "Broom" is *rotem*, a desert shrub with roots that are eaten in famine.
- **30:5** "They are driven out from the community" says that these people were outcasts. "As at a thief" means that the people shouted at them.
- **30:7** "They bray" is the noise of wild donkeys. "Under the nettles they huddle together" is a figure of the poor in the wilderness.
- **30:8** "A senseless, nameless brood" is literally "sons of the fool, and sons of no name".
- **30:9** "I have become their song" and "a byword" repeat the figure of 17:6.
- **30:10** "They do not hold back spit from my face" says that they spit at him.
- **30:11** "God has loosed my bowstring and humbled me" follows the reading tradition (*yitri*, "my cord"). The written text has "his cord". The figure may be of a bow whose string has been loosed, so that the owner is disarmed.
- **30:12** "The rabble" translates the rare word *pirchach*. "Siege-ramps" is *orechot eidam*, "ways of their calamity".
- **30:14** "As through a wide breach they come" is a figure of a city attacked.
- **30:15** "My honour is pursued as by the wind" is a figure of honour that vanishes. "My prosperity" is Hebrew *yeshu'ati*, "my deliverance" or "my safety".
- **30:16** "My soul is poured out within me" uses *nefesh*. See entry H-NEPHESH.
- **30:18** "God grasps my clothing" is hard in the Hebrew. It may be that God has taken hold of Job's garment, or that his garment has been twisted out of shape.
- **30:19** "Dust and ashes" is a figure of low estate (see Genesis 18:27).
- **30:20** "You only look at me" says that God sees him but does not answer. The verb *tit-bonen* means "to consider".
- **30:21** "You have turned cruel to me" is rendered as written. It is a direct charge against God.
- **30:22** "The roar of the storm" translates the hard word *tushiyyah*.
- **30:23** "The house appointed for all the living" is the grave.
- **30:24** "No one lays a hand on a ruin" is hard. It may say that no one strikes a person who is already ruined.
- **30:25** "Did I not weep for the one whose day was hard?" says that Job had shown pity. "My soul" is *nefesh*.
- **30:27** "My inward parts" are Hebrew *me'ai*, "my bowels", the seat of feeling.
- **30:28** "Blackened, but not by the sun" may mean dark with grief or with disease. The word *qoder* means "dark" or "mourning".
- **30:29** "A brother of jackals and a companion of ostriches" refers to animals that cry in the night (see Micah 1:8).
- **30:31** "My lyre is turned to mourning" says that the joy of his life has become a lament.
- **31:1** "A covenant" is Hebrew *berit*. See entry H-BERIT. Job speaks of an agreement that he made with his own eyes. "A young woman" is Hebrew *betulah*, a girl of marriageable age.
- **31:2** "What would be my portion from God above?" asks what God would give to one who did such things.
- **31:5** The oath of chapter 31 uses the form "If I have done this, then let this happen to me". It is a legal form of self-curse. The verses 5 to 40 list sins that Job denies.
- **31:6** "Let me be weighed in a just balance" says that he is willing to be tested. "Integrity" is Hebrew *tummah*.
- **31:7** "My heart has gone after my eyes" repeats the warning of Numbers 15:39.
- **31:9** "Enticed to a woman" is the first oath, against adultery. "I have lain in wait at my neighbour's door" describes an adulterer.
- **31:10** "Let my wife grind for another" is the curse that he will lose his wife to another man. "Grind" may be work as a servant, or a sexual figure, and "let others kneel over her" is a sexual idiom. It is rendered as written.
- **31:11** "A shameful act" is Hebrew *zimmah*, a word for a sexual crime (see Leviticus 18:17). "Punished by judges" says that it is a crime for the courts.
- **31:12** "A fire that consumes to Abaddon" is the figure of Proverbs 6:27-29. "Abaddon" is the place of destruction (see 26:6).
- **31:13** "If I have rejected the claim of my male or female servant" says that he gave his slaves a fair hearing. The Hebrew word is *mishpat*.
- **31:15** "Did not he who made me in the womb make them?" is the ground of the claim of equal humanity (see Proverbs 14:31 and 22:2).
- **31:16** "The eyes of the widow to fail" means that she waited in vain.
- **31:18** The verse is hard in the Hebrew. It may mean that from his youth Job raised the orphan as a father, and led the widow from his mother's womb, which is a figure of lifelong care.
- **31:20** "His body did not bless me" translates *chalatsav*, "his loins".
- **31:21** "I saw I had help in the gate" says that Job had friends in court who would support him if he cheated an orphan.
- **31:22** "Let my shoulder fall from the shoulder blade" is a curse on the arm that raised the hand against the orphan.
- **31:23** "I was in dread of calamity from God" gives the reason for his conduct.
- **31:24** "If I have made gold my trust" is the oath against putting trust in money (see Proverbs 11:28).
- **31:26** "If I have looked at the sun when it shone, or the moon moving in splendour" refers to the worship of the heavenly bodies. "My mouth has kissed my hand" (verse 27) is a gesture of adoration toward them.
- **31:28** "False to God above" translates *ki-chichashti la-El mi-ma'al*, "for I would have denied God on high".
- **31:29** "If I have rejoiced at the ruin of him who hated me" is the oath against rejoicing in an enemy's fall (see Proverbs 24:17).
- **31:30** "By asking for his life with a curse" says that he did not even say a curse against his enemy.
- **31:31** "The men of my tent have not said, 'Who is there who has not been filled with his meat?'" refers to his hospitality. The household would say that all were fed.
- **31:33** "As others do" translates Hebrew *ke-Adam*, which may be "as Adam did" or "as people do". "Hiding my iniquity in my heart" says that he did not hide his wrongs.
- **31:35** "Oh, that I had one to hear me!" is the climax of the oath. "My signature" translates Hebrew *tavi*, from *taw*, the last letter of the alphabet, a mark or sign. "Let the Almighty answer me" is his demand for a reply. "My accuser" is literally "the man of my case" (*ish rivi*). "Indictment" is *sefer*, a written document.
- **31:36** "I would wear it on my shoulder ... like a crown" says that he would be proud to display the charge.
- **31:37** "Like a prince I would approach him" says that Job would go to God without fear.
- **31:38** The verses 38 to 40 may be out of order. Some scholars place them after verse 8 or verse 22. The WABV text keeps the Hebrew order. "My land has cried out against me" refers to wrongly taken land (see Genesis 4:10).
- **31:39** "Eaten its yield without payment" says that he did not use the harvest without paying for it. "Breathe their last" translates *hippachti nefesh*, "breathed out the life of".
- **31:40** "Thorns instead of wheat and stinkweed instead of barley" echoes Genesis 3:18. "The words of Job are ended" is the closing formula (see Psalm 72:20). It ends Job's speeches. His only words from now on are in 40:4-5 and 42:2-6.
- **32:1** "These three men stopped answering Job" ends the dialogue between Job and his three friends. "He was righteous in his own eyes" is Hebrew *hu tsaddiq be-einav*, the reason that is given. A new speaker, Elihu, now comes in. He has not been named before, and he is not named again in the book.
- **32:2** "Elihu son of Barachel the Buzite, of the family of Ram" is the new speaker. His name means "He is my God". Buz is a place or a tribe of the region of Edom and Arabia (see Genesis 22:21 and Jeremiah 25:23). "Because he justified himself rather than God" says that Job put his own claim before God's. The Hebrew is literally "because he justified his soul more than God".
- **32:3** "And yet had condemned Job" is the Hebrew text. The tradition of the scribes says that the original read "and had condemned God", and that the scribes changed the line out of respect. The WABV text follows the Hebrew and is flagged for the scholar.
- **32:4** "Because they were older than he" says that Elihu waited out of respect for age.
- **32:6** "I am young in years" begins Elihu's first speech (32:6 to 33:33). Elihu speaks at length about himself before he speaks to Job.
- **32:7** "Let days speak, and many years teach wisdom" is the usual view that age gives wisdom, which Elihu says he had held.
- **32:8** "The spirit in a person, the breath of the Almighty, that gives understanding" uses *ruach* and *nishmat Shaddai*. See entry H-RUACH. Elihu says that understanding comes from God's gift and not from age.
- **32:9** "It is not the old who are wise" is literally "not the many" (*rabbim*), which may mean "the great" or "the old".
- **32:13** "Do not say, 'We have found wisdom. God may rout him, not a man'" is hard. It says that the friends should not claim that God alone could answer Job.
- **32:15** "They are dismayed. They answer no more" turns from Job to the friends. Elihu speaks of them in the third person.
- **32:18** "I am full of words. The spirit within me constrains me" uses *ruach*. See entry H-RUACH. "Constrains" translates the verb *tsarar*, "squeezes".
- **32:19** "My belly is like wine that has no vent" is a figure of a wineskin that is about to burst.
- **32:21** "I will not show partiality to any person" is a claim to fairness. "Flatter" is Hebrew *kinneh*, to give a title of honour.
- **33:1** "But now, Job, hear my speech" is the first time that Job is addressed by name since the opening of the book. Elihu does so repeatedly (33:31; 34:5, 7, 35, 36; 35:16; 37:14).
- **33:4** "The spirit of God has made me, and the breath of the Almighty gives me life" refers to Genesis 2:7. "Spirit" is *ruach*. See entry H-RUACH.
- **33:6** "I too was pinched off from clay" translates a verb (*qortsti*) from the work of a potter who pinches off a piece of clay. It says that he and Job are alike human.
- **33:7** "No fear of me need terrify you" answers Job's wish in 9:34 that God's dread should not terrify him.
- **33:8** The verses 8 to 11 give Elihu's summary of what Job has said. Job did not say these words exactly. He said that he was in the right (see 9:21; 10:7; 13:18; 16:17; 23:10-12; 27:5-6) and that God treated him as an enemy (13:24, 27). He did not say that he was "pure, without transgression" in these terms.
- **33:12** "In this you are not right" is Elihu's first reply. "God is greater than a human" says that God cannot be accused.
- **33:13** "Saying he will answer none of my words" refers to Job's complaint that God does not answer (see 9:3 and 31:35).
- **33:14** "God speaks in one way, and in two" begins a description of the ways in which God speaks: in dreams (verses 15 to 18) and in sickness (verses 19 to 22).
- **33:16** "Terrifies them with warnings" translates a hard phrase (*ve-al mosaram yachtom*), which may mean "seals their instruction".
- **33:17** "Conceal pride from a man" is literally "hide pride from a man".
- **33:18** "The pit" is Hebrew *shachat*, the grave. "His soul" is *nefesh*. See entry H-NEPHESH.
- **33:19** "Man is also rebuked with pain on his bed" is the second way. Suffering is described as God's instruction.
- **33:22** "Those who bring death" translates *memitim*, which may mean the agents of death or angels of destruction.
- **33:23** "An angel, a mediator, one of the thousand" translates Hebrew *mal'akh melits ehad mini-alef*. The *melits* is an interpreter or intercessor. The figure answers Job's longing for an arbiter (9:33) and a witness in heaven (16:19-21), and in 19:25 for a redeemer. "To declare to man what is right for him" is the mediator's task.
- **33:24** "I have found a ransom" translates Hebrew *matsati khofer*. See entry H-KAPPER. The *kofer* is the price paid to free a person from death (see Exodus 21:30 and Psalm 49:7-8). The text does not say what the ransom is.
- **33:25** "Let his flesh become fresh with youth" describes the healing of a sick person.
- **33:26** "He restores to man his righteousness" translates *va-yashev le-enosh tsidqato*. See entry H-TSEDAQAH.
- **33:27** "He sings before others and says" begins the confession of the one who is healed (verses 27 and 28). "It was not repaid to me" says that he did not get what he deserved.
- **33:28** "He has redeemed my soul" uses *nefesh* and the verb *padah*. See entry H-NEPHESH.
- **33:29** "Twice, three times" says that God gives repeated chances.
- **33:32** "I desire to justify you" translates Hebrew *chafetsti tsadqekha*. It says that Elihu wishes to declare Job in the right, if Job has an answer.
- **34:1** "Then Elihu answered" begins the second speech. Elihu speaks to the friends ("you wise men"), and not to Job.
- **34:3** "The ear tests words as the palate tastes food" repeats 12:11.
- **34:5** "I am in the right, and God has taken away my right" is Elihu's summary of Job's words in 27:2 and 13:18.
- **34:6** "In spite of my right I am counted a liar" is hard in the Hebrew. "My wound is incurable" translates *chitsi*, which is literally "my arrow" and may mean the wound made by an arrow.
- **34:7** "Who drinks up scoffing like water" is similar to the description of the wicked in 15:16.
- **34:9** "It profits a man nothing that he should take delight in God" is not said by Job in these words. It is Elihu's conclusion from Job's argument that God treats the innocent and the wicked alike (9:22; 21:15).
- **34:10** "Far be it from God that he should do wickedness" begins Elihu's defence of God's justice (verses 10 to 15).
- **34:11** "According to a man's work he repays him" is the teaching of retribution that Job has denied.
- **34:14** "If he should set his heart to it and gather to himself his spirit and his breath" uses *ruach* and *neshamah*. See entry H-RUACH. The figure is of God withdrawing the breath of life (see Psalm 104:29).
- **34:17** "Shall one who hates justice govern?" says that God, who rules, cannot be unjust. "Righteous and mighty" is Hebrew *tsaddiq kabbir*.
- **34:18** "Worthless one" is Hebrew *beliya'al*.
- **34:19** "They are all the work of his hands" says that God made the rich and the poor alike.
- **34:20** "The mighty are taken away without human hand" says that God removes them without any human help.
- **34:22** "There is no gloom or deep darkness where evildoers may hide themselves" answers Eliphaz's charge in 22:13-14 and Job's complaint in 24:13-17.
- **34:24** "He shatters the mighty without investigation" says that God does not need a trial to know the facts.
- **34:26** "In a place for all to see" is hard (*bimqom ro'im*, "in the place of onlookers").
- **34:29** "When he is quiet, who can condemn?" is hard. It says that God's silence cannot be judged.
- **34:33** "Will he then make repayment to suit you?" is hard in the Hebrew. It seems to ask whether God must repay on Job's terms.
- **34:36** "Would that Job were tried to the end" translates a hard word (*avi*), which may be "my father" or "would that". The sense is that Job should be tested to the limit.
- **34:37** "He claps his hands among us" is a gesture of mockery. "He adds rebellion to his sin" says that Elihu counts Job's words as rebellion.
- **35:2** "It is my right before God" is Elihu's account of Job's claim. "What advantage have I?" repeats 34:9.
- **35:5** "Look at the heavens and see" begins Elihu's argument that human goodness and sin do not affect God (verses 5 to 8). It is like the argument of Eliphaz in 22:2-3 and goes against Job's question in 7:20.
- **35:9** "Because of the multitude of oppressions people cry out" says that Elihu knows of the cry of the oppressed, but that they do not turn to God.
- **35:10** "Who gives songs in the night" translates Hebrew *noten zemirot ba-laylah*. The word *zemirot* may mean "songs" or "strength". It is a line of great beauty.
- **35:11** "Who teaches us more than the beasts of the earth and makes us wiser than the birds of the heavens" refers to 12:7-8.
- **35:12** "He does not answer, because of the pride of evil men" is Elihu's reason for God's silence.
- **35:13** "An empty cry" is Hebrew *shav*, "emptiness".
- **35:14** "That the case is before him, and you are waiting for him" refers to Job's words in 23:3-9 and 31:35.
- **35:15** "His anger does not punish, and he does not take much note of folly" is hard. The word for "folly" (*pash*) is rare. It says that God has not yet punished Job.
- **35:16** "Job opens his mouth in empty talk" ends the chapter with Elihu's verdict.
- **36:1** "And Elihu continued" begins the fourth speech (36:1 to 37:24). It is the last speech before the LORD speaks.
- **36:2** "On God's behalf" translates *le-Eloah millim*, "there are still words for God". Elihu claims to speak for God.
- **36:3** "I will get my knowledge from afar" means from far away or from a wide range. "Ascribe righteousness to my Maker" is the aim of the speech. The word is *tsedeq*. See entry H-TSEDAQAH.
- **36:4** "One who is perfect in knowledge is with you" is bold. The phrase *tamim de'ot* is used of God in 37:16. Here it may be a claim about Elihu himself.
- **36:5** "God is mighty, and does not despise anyone" begins a description of God's care for the afflicted and the righteous (verses 5 to 12).
- **36:8** "If they are bound in fetters and caught in the cords of affliction" says that suffering may be a form of discipline.
- **36:10** "He opens their ears to instruction" uses the word *musar*, discipline. "Return from iniquity" translates *shuv*. See entry H-SHUV.
- **36:12** "They die without knowledge" is the fate of those who do not listen.
- **36:14** "Cult prostitutes" translates Hebrew *qedeshim*, male temple prostitutes (see Deuteronomy 23:17 and 1 Kings 14:24). The line says that the godless die young and in shame.
- **36:15** "He delivers the afflicted by their affliction" is Elihu's chief teaching, that God uses pain to bring the sufferer to himself.
- **36:16** "He also allured you out of distress into a broad place" says that God would have led Job out of trouble (see Psalm 18:19 and Hosea 2:14). "Full of fatness" is a figure of rich food.
- **36:17** "You are full of the judgement on the wicked" is hard. It may say that Job is occupied with the judgement that the wicked deserve.
- **36:18** "Beware lest wrath entice you into scoffing" is a warning to Job. "The greatness of the ransom" translates *rov kofer*. See entry H-KAPPER. The sense is that the greatness of the price should not turn him aside.
- **36:20** "Do not long for the night" may refer to the night of death or of judgement, when "peoples vanish".
- **36:21** "Do not turn to iniquity, for this you have preferred to affliction" says that Elihu accuses Job of preferring protest to submission.
- **36:22** "Who is a teacher like him?" translates *moreh*. Elihu says that God is the true teacher.
- **36:24** "Remember to extol his work" is a call to praise. "Of which people have sung" refers to the praises of God's works.
- **36:26** "God is great, and we know him not" begins the hymn on the storm (36:26 to 37:13). "The number of his years is unsearchable" says that God is without age.
- **36:27** "He draws up the drops of water" begins a description of rain. "His mist" is the water that rises from the earth (see Genesis 2:6).
- **36:29** "The thunderings of his pavilion" translates *teshu'ot sukkato*. The sky is God's tent.
- **36:30** "He scatters his lightning about him and covers the roots of the sea" is hard. The Hebrew *shorshe ha-yam* may be "the depths of the sea".
- **36:31** "For by these he judges peoples" says that the storm may bring punishment, or food.
- **36:32** "He covers his hands with the lightning and commands it to strike the mark" is a figure of God as an archer or a thrower.
- **36:33** "Its crashing declares his presence, and the cattle also declare that he rises" is a very hard verse. The Hebrew may also be read "its noise tells of him; the cattle too of the one who comes up". The sense is that thunder and animals alike announce the storm.
- **37:1** "My heart trembles" says that Elihu is afraid of the storm.
- **37:2** "The thunder of his voice" refers to the voice of God in the storm (see Psalm 29).
- **37:4** "He does not restrain the lightnings" translates a rare verb (*ye'aqqevem*).
- **37:5** "God thunders wondrously with his voice" recalls Psalm 29. "Great things that we cannot comprehend" is the theme of the hymn.
- **37:6** "To the snow he says, 'Fall on the earth'" shows God's word as a command to the weather.
- **37:7** "He seals up the hand of every man" says that winter stops work, so that all people know what God does.
- **37:9** "From its chamber comes the whirlwind" refers to the store of the wind. "The scattering winds" are the north winds.
- **37:10** "By the breath of God ice is given" uses *neshamat El*. See entry H-RUACH.
- **37:13** "Whether for correction, or for his land, or for love" gives three purposes of the storm: *shevet* ("rod"), his land, and *chesed*. "Love" is *chesed*. See entry H-HESED.
- **37:14** "Hear this, Job" begins the closing questions of Elihu (verses 14 to 24), which are in the style of the questions that the LORD asks in chapters 38 to 41.
- **37:16** "The balancings of the clouds" says that the clouds are poised by God. "Him who is perfect in knowledge" is God.
- **37:17** "Your garments are hot when the earth is still because of the south wind" says that the heat of summer is an example of God's power.
- **37:18** "Hard as a cast metal mirror" shows the sky as a solid sheet (see Genesis 1:6). The word *raqia* is not used here.
- **37:19** "Teach us what we shall say to him" is Elihu's request. "Because of darkness" says that human understanding is dim.
- **37:20** "Shall it be told him that I would speak? Did a man ever wish that he would be swallowed up?" is hard. It says that no one would dare to demand a hearing, since to speak to God would be fatal.
- **37:22** "Out of the north comes golden splendour" translates Hebrew *mi-tsafon zahav ye'eteh*. The word *tsafon* is the north, the place of the mountain of God, and *zahav* is "gold". It may refer to the glow of the sky, or to the light of God's presence. God "is clothed with awesome majesty".
- **37:23** "The Almighty, we cannot find him" repeats Job's words in 23:8-9. "He will not violate" translates *lo ye'anneh*, which may mean "he does not afflict" or "he does not answer". The sense is that God does not do injustice.
- **37:24** "He does not regard any who are wise in their own conceit" ends Elihu's speeches. The phrase *chakhme-lev* is "the wise of heart". The next words in the book are the LORD's (38:1).
- **38:1** "The LORD answered Job out of the whirlwind" uses the divine name *YHWH*. It is the first time since the prologue that the LORD speaks. "The whirlwind" is Hebrew *se'arah*, a storm wind (see 1 Kings 19:11 and Ezekiel 1:4). Job had said that God "crushes me with a tempest" (9:17). The LORD does not answer Job's charges, does not explain his suffering, and does not mention the adversary or the matter of the prologue. He asks questions.
- **38:2** "Who is this that darkens counsel by words without knowledge?" is the first question. "Counsel" is Hebrew *etsah*, God's plan. The charge is almost the same as Elihu's charge of Job in 34:35. The LORD speaks to Job and not to Elihu. Nothing in the text says whom the question is about, though the LORD addresses Job in verse 3.
- **38:3** "Brace yourself like a man" is literally "gird up your loins like a man" (*ezor na ke-gever chalatsekha*), the words for a person who is about to fight or run. "I will question you, and you will answer me" is the formula of a summons to court. It is repeated in 40:7.
- **38:4** "Where were you when I laid the foundation of the earth?" begins the series of questions about creation (verses 4 to 38). The questions are rendered as questions.
- **38:5** "Who set its measurements? Surely you know" is irony. The creation is shown as a building, with a measuring line.
- **38:7** "When the morning stars sang together and all the sons of God shouted for joy" refers to the heavenly beings at creation. "The sons of God" is Hebrew *benei Elohim*, the same beings as in 1:6 and 2:1. The two lines say the same thing in parallel.
- **38:8** "Who shut in the sea with doors when it burst out from the womb?" pictures the sea as a newborn that is given limits. The old stories show the sea as a power to be defeated (see 7:12 and 26:12).
- **38:9** "I made clouds its garment and thick darkness its swaddling band" is a figure of a child wrapped in cloth. The first words, "I made", show that the LORD speaks in the first person here.
- **38:11** "Thus far shall you come, and no farther, and here shall your proud waves be stopped" is the limit set on the sea.
- **38:12** "Have you commanded the morning since your days began?" asks whether Job ever gave an order to the dawn.
- **38:13** "That it might take hold of the skirts of the earth, and the wicked be shaken out of it" pictures the dawn as a hand that shakes the earth like a garment, so that the wicked are shaken out. The image connects the order of the world with justice.
- **38:14** "It is changed like clay under the seal" refers to the way a seal makes a clay surface take shape. The earth takes its shape at dawn.
- **38:15** "From the wicked their light is withheld" refers to the darkness in which the wicked work (see 24:13-17).
- **38:16** "The springs of the sea" are the sources of the ocean below the earth. "The recesses of the deep" are its hidden places.
- **38:17** "The gates of death" and "the gates of deep darkness" refer to the entrance to Sheol (see 10:21-22 and Psalm 9:13).
- **38:21** "You know, for you were born then, and the number of your days is great" is irony, since Job was not there.
- **38:22** "The storehouses of the snow ... the storehouses of the hail" shows the weather as stored in God's armoury. "The day of battle and war" refers to the use of hail against enemies (see Exodus 9:18-26 and Joshua 10:11).
- **38:24** "The place where the light is distributed" is hard. The word for "light" (*or*) may here mean "lightning" or "mist". "The east wind" is the hot wind of the desert.
- **38:26** "To bring rain on a land where no one lives, on the desert in which there is no man" says that God's care reaches places where no human is. It is a striking part of the speech, since the world is not centred on Job or on human beings.
- **38:28** "Has the rain a father?" is a figure that shows the rain, the dew and the ice as born.
- **38:31** "The Pleiades" is Hebrew *Kimah* and "Orion" is *Kesil*. The names are traditional (see 9:9 and Amos 5:8).
- **38:32** "The constellations" translates Hebrew *Mazzarot*, a word that occurs only here and may mean "constellations" or a particular group of stars. "The Bear with its children" is Hebrew *Ayish*, probably the Great Bear and its three stars.
- **38:33** "The ordinances of the heavens" are the laws of the stars. "Their rule on the earth" says that the stars affect the earth.
- **38:35** "Can you send forth lightnings, that they may go and say to you, 'Here we are'?" shows the lightning as servants who report to their master.
- **38:36** "The inward parts" and "the mind" translate two rare words (*tuchot* and *sekhvi*). They may also mean two birds (the ibis and the cock), which in Egyptian lore were symbols of wisdom. The WABV text follows the sense "inward parts" and "mind" and the footnote gives the other sense.
- **38:37** "Who can tilt the waterskins of the heavens?" is a figure of the rain as water poured from jars.
- **38:39** "Can you hunt the prey for the lion?" begins the series of questions about wild animals (38:39 to 39:30). Each animal lives apart from humans, and each is cared for by the LORD.
- **38:41** "Its young ones cry to God for help" says that the young ravens call to God (*El*).
- **39:1** "The mountain goats" and "the does" are wild animals whose births no one watches.
- **39:5** "The wild donkey" is free, as the LORD has made it. "The arid plain" is Hebrew *aravah*.
- **39:9** "The wild ox" is Hebrew *re'em*, the aurochs, a large wild bull that is now extinct. It is not a unicorn.
- **39:13** "The pinions and plumage of love" translates a hard line. The Hebrew word *chasidah* means "the faithful one" and is also the name of the stork. The line may read "are they the pinions and plumage of the stork?" The WABV text keeps the sense "love" and the footnote gives "stork". The contrast is between the ostrich and the stork, which is known for care of its young.
- **39:15** "Forgetting that a foot may crush them" says that the ostrich does not guard her eggs.
- **39:16** "She deals cruelly with her young as if they were not hers" is rendered as written.
- **39:17** "God has made her forget wisdom and given her no share in understanding" is rendered as written. It says that the LORD made the ostrich without wisdom, and shows that the LORD's creation includes creatures that seem foolish. The sentence is one of the most striking in the speech.
- **39:18** "She laughs at the horse and his rider" says that the ostrich is faster than the horse.
- **39:19** "Mane" translates Hebrew *ra'amah*, a rare word that may mean "mane" or "thunder".
- **39:20** "Leap like the locust" is a figure of the horse's springing. "His majestic snorting is terrifying" refers to the war-horse.
- **39:25** "He says 'Aha!'" translates *he'ach*, a sound of eagerness. "He smells the battle from afar" is a figure of the horse's keenness.
- **39:26** "The hawk" is Hebrew *nets*, a bird of prey. "Toward the south" refers to its migration.
- **39:27** "The eagle" is Hebrew *nesher*, which may also mean the griffon vulture.
- **39:30** "Where the slain are, there is he" says that the eagle feeds on the dead. The line is taken up in Matthew 24:28 and Luke 17:37.
- **40:1** "The LORD said to Job" begins the LORD's challenge, which follows the first speech (chapters 38 and 39).
- **40:2** "A faultfinder" translates a rare verb (*yissor*), which may mean "one who corrects" or "disputes". "Him who accuses God" is Hebrew *mokhiach Eloah*, the one who argues a case against God (see 9:33 and 13:3). The LORD asks for an answer to the whole of what he has said.
- **40:3** "Job answered the LORD" begins Job's first short reply (verses 3 to 5).
- **40:4** "I am of small account" translates Hebrew *qallothi*, "I am slight" or "I am too light". "I lay my hand on my mouth" is the gesture of silence (see 21:5 and 29:9).
- **40:5** "I have spoken once, and I will not answer. Twice, but I will proceed no further" is a numerical form. Job is silent, but he does not say that he was wrong. The LORD continues.
- **40:6** "The LORD answered Job out of the whirlwind" repeats 38:1 and begins the second speech (40:6 to 41:34).
- **40:7** "Brace yourself like a man" repeats 38:3.
- **40:8** "Will you even put me in the wrong?" translates Hebrew *ha-af tafer mishpati*, "will you even break my justice?". "Will you condemn me that you may be in the right?" names the issue of the book. The LORD says that Job has treated God's justice as a thing to be tested. The verse is rendered as written.
- **40:9** "Have you an arm like God, and can you thunder with a voice like his?" asks whether Job has God's power.
- **40:10** "Adorn yourself with majesty and dignity" begins an ironic invitation to take God's place (verses 10 to 14).
- **40:11** "Pour out the overflowings of your anger" invites Job to judge the proud as he thinks God should.
- **40:13** "Bind their faces in the world below" translates a hard word (*taman*), "in the hidden place". The line refers to Sheol or the grave.
- **40:14** "Then I will also acknowledge to you that your own right hand can save you" says that only if Job could do what God does would he be able to save himself.
- **40:15** "Behemoth" is Hebrew *behemot*, the plural of *behemah*, "beast". It is a great beast. Tradition identifies it with the hippopotamus, and some suggest the elephant. Some scholars think it is a mythic creature. "Which I made as I made you" says that Behemoth is Job's fellow creature. "He eats grass like an ox" is the one plain statement of its habits.
- **40:16** "The muscles of his belly" translates *shririm*, a rare word.
- **40:17** "He makes his tail stiff like a cedar" translates a hard line. The hippopotamus has a short tail, so some scholars think that the word *zanav* is a euphemism or that the figure is exaggeration.
- **40:19** "He is the first of the works of God" is literally "the first of the ways of God" (*reshit darkei El*). "Let him who made him bring near his sword" says that only his maker can approach him. The Hebrew is hard.
- **40:21** "Lotus plants" translates Hebrew *tse'elim*, a water plant.
- **40:23** "Though the Jordan rushes against his mouth" is a figure of confidence. The hippopotamus is not found in the Jordan, so the line may be an exaggeration.
- **40:24** "Take him by his eyes" may be "take him while he watches". "Pierce his nose with a snare" is hard (*moqshim*). The questions say that no one can take him.
- **41:1** In the Hebrew Bible 41:1-8 is numbered 40:25-32, so that the Hebrew chapter 41 has twenty-six verses (the English verses 9 to 34) and the English chapter 41 has thirty-four. "Leviathan" is Hebrew *livyatan*. It is the sea monster of the old stories, which the LORD defeats (see Psalm 74:14, Isaiah 27:1 and Job 3:8). In later tradition it is the crocodile or a great sea creature. The description here has mythic details.
- **41:2** "A rope in his nose" and "a hook" in the jaw refer to the way captive animals were led (see 2 Kings 19:28 and Ezekiel 29:4).
- **41:3** "Will he make many pleas to you?" says that Leviathan would not beg for mercy.
- **41:4** "A covenant" is Hebrew *berit*. See entry H-BERIT. The line asks whether Leviathan would make a treaty to be Job's servant.
- **41:5** "Will you play with him as with a bird?" turns Psalm 104:26 around. There God made Leviathan to play. "A leash for your girls" is a figure of a pet.
- **41:6** "Traders" translates Hebrew *chavverim*, "partners", and "merchants" is *kena'anim*, "Canaanites".
- **41:8** "Remember the battle. You will not do it again" is hard. It may say that one fight would be enough.
- **41:9** "The hope of a man is false" is the Hebrew 41:1. The sight of Leviathan lays a person low.
- **41:10** "Who then is he who can stand before me?" follows the Hebrew, in which the LORD says "before me". Some manuscripts have "before him".
- **41:11** "Who has first given to me, that I should repay him?" is quoted in Romans 11:35. "Whatever is under the whole heaven is mine" is the LORD's claim.
- **41:12** "I will not keep silent concerning his limbs" begins a long description of the body of Leviathan (verses 12 to 34). The Hebrew of the line is hard. It is a statement of the LORD about Leviathan's "goodly frame".
- **41:13** "His outer garment" is the skin. "His double coat of mail" translates a hard word (*resen*), which may mean "bridle" or "double bridle".
- **41:14** "The doors of his face" are his jaws.
- **41:15** "Rows of shields" translates Hebrew *afiqei maginnim*, "channels of shields", the scales.
- **41:18** "His sneezings flash forth light" is a figure of the spray that catches the sun. "His eyes are like the eyelids of the dawn" says that his eyes glow red.
- **41:19** "Flaming torches" and "sparks of fire" are the fire of a dragon. The details have been taken as poetry or as a mythic picture.
- **41:20** "As from a boiling pot and burning rushes" is hard (*dud nafuach ve-agmon*).
- **41:22** "Terror dances before him" translates the rare verb *tadutz*.
- **41:24** "Hard as the lower millstone" refers to the stone that does not move in a mill.
- **41:25** "At the crashing they are beside themselves" translates hard words (*mi-shevarim yitchatta'u*).
- **41:26** "The spear, the dart, or the javelin" translates three rare words for weapons (*massa*, *chanit* and *shiryah*). They are weapons that do not harm him.
- **41:30** "His underparts are like sharp potsherds" says that his belly has hard scales. "A threshing sledge" is Hebrew *charuts*, a board with stones that is dragged over grain.
- **41:31** "A pot of ointment" translates *merqachah*, a pot in which perfume is boiled.
- **41:32** "One would think the deep to be white-haired" is a figure of the foam in his wake.
- **41:33** "A creature without fear" translates a hard phrase (*he-asuy li-vli chat*, "made without terror").
- **41:34** "King over all the sons of pride" translates *melekh al kol bene shachats*. See 28:8. The LORD calls Leviathan a king of the proud.
- **42:1** "Job answered the LORD" begins Job's second reply (verses 1 to 6).
- **42:2** "I know that you can do all things" is Job's confession of God's power. "No purpose of yours can be thwarted" translates Hebrew *mezimmah*, "plan".
- **42:3** "Who is this that hides counsel without knowledge?" quotes the LORD's question in 38:2. Job's version has "hides" (*ma'lim*) where the LORD said "darkens" (*machshikh*). "Things too wonderful for me" is Job's admission.
- **42:4** "Hear, and I will speak. I will question you, and you will answer me" quotes the LORD's words in 38:3 and 40:7.
- **42:5** "I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you" is the turning point. The text does not say what Job sees. It may refer to his sight of the LORD in the whirlwind. It recalls his hope in 19:26-27 that his own eyes would see God.
- **42:6** "Therefore I despise myself, and repent in dust and ashes" is among the most disputed lines in the book. The first verb (*em'as*) has no object, so that it may mean "I despise myself", "I despise what I said" or "I retract". The second verb (*nicham*) may mean "repent", "be sorry", "change my mind" or "be comforted". See entry H-NACHAM. "Dust and ashes" may refer to the place of mourning, to human frailty, or to both. The text does not say that Job confesses to the sins that the friends charged him with, and in verse 7 the LORD says that he has spoken rightly. The WABV text keeps the ambiguity as far as the English allows.
- **42:7** "After the LORD had spoken these words to Job" begins the epilogue, which is prose. "The LORD said to Eliphaz the Temanite" addresses the first of the friends. "My anger burns against you and against your two friends" is a strong judgement. "You have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has" is one of the most important lines in the book. The Hebrew word *nekhonah* means "right" or "established", and the phrase *elai* may mean "of me" or "to me". The text does not say which of Job's words were right. It overturns the teaching of the friends that suffering is the sure result of sin.
- **42:8** "Seven bulls and seven rams" is an offering of a large size (see Numbers 23:1). "My servant Job shall pray for you" makes Job the one who intercedes for his friends. "I will accept his prayer" translates *panav essa*, "I will lift his face". "Folly" is Hebrew *nevalah*.
- **42:9** "Did what the LORD had told them" says that the friends obeyed.
- **42:10** "The LORD restored the fortunes of Job" translates the idiom *shav et-shevut Iyyov* (see Psalm 126:1). It comes "when he had prayed for his friends". "Twice as much as he had before" is Hebrew *mishneh*.
- **42:11** "His brothers and sisters and all who had known him before" come only now. "They ate bread with him" is a sign of fellowship. "For all the evil that the LORD had brought on him" is the narrator's statement that the LORD brought the evil, which is rendered as written. "A piece of silver" translates *qesitah*, a unit of money of uncertain value (see Genesis 33:19). "A gold ring" is Hebrew *nezem*.
- **42:12** "The LORD blessed the latter days of Job more than his beginning" is followed by numbers that are twice those of 1:3.
- **42:13** "Seven sons and three daughters" is the same number as in 1:2. The children are not doubled, and the text does not say that the loss of the first ten is made good.
- **42:14** "Jemimah ... Keziah ... Keren-happuch" are the names of the daughters. They mean "dove" (or "day"), "cassia" and "horn of eye-paint". The sons are not named.
- **42:15** "Their father gave them an inheritance among their brothers" is unusual, since daughters did not normally inherit (see Numbers 27:1-11).
- **42:16** "A hundred and forty years" may be twice the span of seventy (Psalm 90:10). "Four generations" means Job's children and grandchildren.
- **42:17** "Old, full of days" is the formula for a good death (see Genesis 25:8). The Greek translation has a long addition at this point, which says that Job will rise again and gives him a family and a place. It is not part of this text.
